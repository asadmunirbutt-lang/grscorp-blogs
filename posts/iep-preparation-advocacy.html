IEP Preparation: Questions to Ask & How to Advocate for Your Child

📅 October 16, 2026 ✍️ AMB 📁 School & Advocacy

The night before your IEP meeting, you're anxious. You know your child's teachers mean well, but you're also aware that the school's priorities aren't always your child's priorities. You want to advocate effectively, but you're not sure what questions to ask or what you're legally entitled to.

You're in a room with six school staff members, one teacher, and you. The power dynamic feels unbalanced. By the end of the meeting, you've agreed to things you didn't fully understand, and you leave feeling like you didn't adequately represent your child.

This happens to too many families. But it doesn't have to be this way. An IEP meeting doesn't have to feel like you're being steamrolled. With proper preparation and knowledge of your rights, you can be an effective advocate for your child.

Understanding What an IEP Is (and Isn't)

Your child's IEP (Individualized Education Program) is a legal document that outlines their educational plan for the year. It's supposed to be collaborative—you and the school working together for your child's benefit.

But here's what many parents don't realize: the school doesn't have to give your child everything they need. They have to provide "free appropriate public education" (FAPE), which is much lower than "best possible education." You need to know the difference.

Before the IEP Meeting: Preparation is Everything

📋 Pre-Meeting Checklist:
  • Gather all testing data (evaluations, assessments, teacher reports)
  • Document concerns with specific examples and dates
  • Bring a support person (advocate, therapist, trusted friend)
  • Review last year's IEP and progress toward goals
  • Write down questions before the meeting
  • Request any reports/evaluations in advance
  • Know your child's specific needs and strengths

Critical Questions to Ask at the IEP Meeting

On Goals

  • "How will we measure progress on this goal?"
  • "What data do you have about my child's current performance?"
  • "Is this goal addressing my child's needs or just what the school can provide?"
  • "How often will progress be monitored?"

On Accommodations

  • "What accommodations does my child need to access the curriculum?"
  • "How will these accommodations be implemented across all settings?"
  • "Can my child have breaks when needed without losing instructional time?"
  • "What about sensory/movement breaks throughout the day?"

On Services

  • "What services does my child need to be successful?"
  • "How often will they receive these services?"
  • "Will services happen in the regular classroom or pull-out?"
  • "How will we know if the current level of service is enough?"

Your Legal Rights (Know These!)

You have the right to:

  • See all evaluations and records before the meeting
  • Bring an advocate or attorney
  • Request independent evaluations if you disagree with school assessments
  • Request changes to the IEP at any time (not just annual meetings)
  • Refuse parts of the IEP while accepting others
  • Ask for specific services or accommodations

Red Flags: When to Push Back

Be cautious if the school:

  • Says "we can't do that" without explaining why
  • Has the same goals as last year despite no progress
  • Minimizes your concerns or dismisses your input
  • Suggests segregating your child without evidence it's necessary
  • Doesn't have data to support their recommendations
  • Pressures you to sign immediately without time to review

How to Be an Effective Advocate

During the Meeting

  • Speak up early: If something concerns you, address it then, not after
  • Ask for clarification: Don't nod along if you don't understand
  • Take notes: Write down what's discussed and agreed to
  • Propose specific solutions: Don't just say "my child needs more support"—say "my child needs 3x weekly speech therapy in the general classroom"
  • Request written responses: If they can't answer a question, ask for a written response within a week

After the Meeting

  • Don't sign immediately. Take it home, review it, think about it
  • Check that everything discussed is in writing
  • Keep copies of everything
  • Follow up in writing if anything was discussed that needs clarification

When to Escalate

If you and the school can't agree:

  1. Request mediation (free, through your state)
  2. File a due process complaint (legal action)
  3. Work with an advocate or attorney (many offer free consultations)

Don't be afraid to escalate if your child's needs aren't being met. That's what these processes are for.
