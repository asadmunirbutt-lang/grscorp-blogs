New York Early Intervention Program: A Complete Parent Guide (Birth to Age 3)

Published: September 13, 2026
By: GRSCorp
Category: NYS Family Resources
Read time: 16 min

You noticed something. Maybe your one-year-old is not babbling the way her cousin did. Maybe your eighteen-month-old lines up his cars for an hour but does not point at the airplane overhead. Maybe your pediatrician said the words "let's keep an eye on it" and something in your chest tightened. Whatever brought you here, you are almost certainly asking the same question every parent asks: what do I actually do right now?

In New York State, the answer is a phone call. The Early Intervention Program (EIP) is a statewide entitlement for children from birth to age three who have a developmental delay or a diagnosed condition likely to cause one. It is one of the most powerful — and most underused — supports available to New York families. It costs families nothing. You do not need a diagnosis. You do not need a doctor's referral. You can pick up the phone yourself, today, and start a clock that the county is legally required to honor.

This guide walks through the whole system: how eligibility actually works, the step-by-step referral process and its hard deadlines, what an IFSP is and how to make it a good one, what services are available, your rights when something goes wrong, and the age-three transition that catches so many families off guard. It is written for New York specifically, because the details matter and generic national advice will not help you at a county meeting.

Why Birth to Three Matters So Much

The case for early intervention is not sentimental — it is neurological. In the first three years of life, the brain builds connections at a rate it will never approach again. Circuits for language, social attention, motor planning, and emotional regulation are laid down through repeated, responsive interaction with caregivers. Connections that get used are strengthened; connections that go unused are pruned away. This is what researchers mean by neuroplasticity, and it is why the same amount of therapy delivered at eighteen months and at six years does not produce the same result.

For autistic children specifically, decades of research converge on a consistent finding: intervention that starts earlier, happens more often, and is embedded in everyday routines with the people the child loves tends to produce better outcomes in communication, social engagement, and adaptive skills than intervention that starts later or happens only in a clinic room. Landmark studies of naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions delivered to toddlers have shown meaningful gains in language and daily living skills, with some children needing substantially less support by school age.

Two things are worth saying clearly, because they are often lost in the enthusiasm.

An honest framing of the evidence

Early intervention genuinely helps, and it is not a race to "fix" your child. The goal is not to make an autistic child non-autistic. The goal is to build communication, reduce frustration, support regulation, and give your child tools to participate in the world on their own terms. Good early intervention respects who your child is while expanding what they can do and say.

A late start is not a lost cause. The brain remains plastic throughout childhood and beyond. If your child is already two and a half, or already three, the right move is to start now — not to grieve the months behind you. Parents who find this system late still get real value from it.

There is also a practical argument. Skills built early cost less to build. A child who develops functional communication at two is less likely to need intensive behavioral support at six because they cannot tell anyone what hurts. Early intervention is, among other things, the least expensive and least restrictive point at which the system will ever meet your family.

What the New York Early Intervention Program Actually Is

The Early Intervention Program is New York's implementation of Part C of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), the federal law that establishes early intervention for infants and toddlers with disabilities. Every state runs a Part C program. New York's is administered at the state level by the New York State Department of Health (DOH), Bureau of Early Intervention, and delivered locally by each county — and by New York City — through a designated official called the Early Intervention Official (EIO).

That structure matters more than it sounds. It means:

  • The program is a health program, not a school program. Your local school district is not involved until the age-three transition.
  • Your county's EIO is legally responsible for your child's services — for arranging them, authorizing them, and making sure they are actually delivered.
  • Rules are set statewide, so a family in Saratoga County and a family in Queens have the same rights, even though local capacity and provider availability differ enormously.
  • Because this is an entitlement under federal law, eligible children are not placed on a waiting list for the right to services. If you encounter a wait, it is a capacity problem the county must solve — not a legal limit on your child's entitlement.

It costs your family nothing. Really.

This is the single most surprising fact about the program, and the one families most often disbelieve. In New York, Early Intervention services are provided at no cost to parents. Specifically:

No fees, no sliding scale, no income test

There is no charge to families for the multidisciplinary evaluation, for service coordination, or for the therapies written into the IFSP. Eligibility does not depend on your income, your assets, or your immigration status. A family earning $400,000 and a family earning $20,000 receive the same entitlement on the same terms.

Insurance is billed — but you are not left holding the bill

New York does bill third-party payers. Providers bill commercial insurance and Medicaid for EI services, and parents are asked to provide insurance information so the state can seek reimbursement. But families are not responsible for copayments, coinsurance, or deductibles attributable to Early Intervention services, and New York law protects EI families from having these services counted against annual or lifetime policy limits. If your insurance denies a claim, that is between the provider, the county, and the insurer. It is not a bill that lands on your kitchen table.

If you ever receive an invoice for an Early Intervention service in your IFSP, do not pay it. Call your service coordinator and your county Early Intervention Official immediately, and ask for the discrepancy in writing. Billing errors happen, and they are resolved between the provider and the county — not by the family.

Families sometimes decline to give insurance information out of fear of costs or premium increases. You can ask your service coordinator to explain exactly how billing works in your county before you sign anything — but understand that the protection exists precisely so that the billing process does not become your problem.

Who Qualifies for Early Intervention in New York

There are two separate doors into the program. A child needs only one of them.

Door 1: A measured developmental delay

New York evaluates development across five functional areas — cognitive, physical (including vision, hearing, and motor), communication, social-emotional, and adaptive (self-help skills). A child is eligible based on delay if the multidisciplinary evaluation documents:

  • A 12-month delay in one functional area (for example, a 24-month-old communicating like a 12-month-old), or
  • A 33% delay in one functional area, or
  • A 25% delay in two or more functional areas, or
  • Scores on standardized instruments that fall a specified distance below the mean — generally at least 2.0 standard deviations below in one area, or 1.5 standard deviations below in two or more areas

Note the second and third criteria carefully. Many families assume their child is "not delayed enough" because no single area looks catastrophic. But a child who is 25% behind in both communication and social-emotional development is eligible, even though neither area alone would qualify. This is why a full multidisciplinary evaluation matters — a speech-only screening can miss eligibility that a broad evaluation would find.

Door 2: A diagnosed condition with a high probability of delay

A child is also eligible if they have a diagnosed physical or mental condition that has a high probability of resulting in developmental delay — even if the child does not currently show a measurable delay. Autism spectrum disorder is such a condition. So are conditions like Down syndrome, cerebral palsy, significant hearing or vision impairment, fragile X syndrome, and a range of genetic and neurological diagnoses.

The most important sentence in this section: you do not need an autism diagnosis to get Early Intervention. A documented delay is entirely sufficient on its own. Diagnostic evaluations in New York often have months-long waits — and there is no reason to sit in that queue doing nothing. Refer your child to Early Intervention now, get services started based on delay, and pursue the diagnostic evaluation in parallel. The two processes are independent, and a later diagnosis can be added to an existing IFSP.

If you are early in the noticing stage, our guide to early signs of autism in young children covers what developmental differences typically look like in the first three years, and how to get an autism evaluation walks through the diagnostic pathway that runs alongside this one.

The Referral Process, Step by Step — and the 45-Day Clock

New York's Part C process is built around one governing deadline: from the date of referral, the county has 45 days to complete the evaluation, determine eligibility, and hold the IFSP meeting for an eligible child. Forty-five days. Not forty-five business days. Everything below happens inside that window.

1
Refer your child to the county Early Intervention Official
Day 0 — the clock starts

Call your county Department of Health or Department of Public Health and ask for the Early Intervention Program. In New York City, contact the NYC Early Intervention Program through 311. Say plainly: "I am referring my child to Early Intervention. I have concerns about their development."

Parents can self-refer. You do not need a pediatrician's referral, a prescription, a diagnosis, or anyone's permission. (Physicians and other primary referral sources are separately obligated to refer children they suspect of having a delay, with parental consent — but you never have to wait for them to do it.) Note the date you called and the name of the person you spoke to. That date is the start of your 45 days.

2
An Initial Service Coordinator is assigned
Within days of referral

The EIO assigns an Initial Service Coordinator (ISC) — your guide through this first phase. The ISC will contact you, explain your rights in writing, obtain your written consent to evaluate, help you choose an evaluator, and arrange the evaluation.

You have the right to choose your evaluator from among the agencies approved in your county, and you have the right to change service coordinators if the relationship is not working. Ask your ISC for their direct phone number and email at the first contact, and use email whenever possible so there is a record.

3
The multidisciplinary evaluation — at no cost
Scheduled within the 45-day window

A team of at least two qualified professionals from different disciplines evaluates your child across all five functional areas, along with a health assessment and a family-directed assessment of your family's resources, priorities, and concerns related to your child's development. The evaluation is free regardless of the outcome — including if your child is found ineligible.

Evaluations should happen in a setting and at a time where your child can show what they can actually do. A tired toddler in an unfamiliar clinic at 4 p.m. is not a representative sample. Ask for a morning appointment, ask whether part of it can happen at home, and tell the evaluators about skills they did not get to see — what you report counts as data.

4
Eligibility determination
After the evaluation, inside the 45 days

The EIO, with the evaluation team, determines whether your child meets the delay or diagnosed-condition criteria. You are entitled to a copy of the full written evaluation report — request it in writing and read it before the IFSP meeting, not at it.

If your child is found ineligible and you disagree, you have real options: you may request an independent look at the data, use mediation, or file for an impartial hearing (see the rights section below). You can also re-refer later if your concerns persist or new ones emerge — development changes fast at this age, and a child found ineligible at 14 months may clearly qualify at 22 months.

5
The IFSP meeting
By day 45

For an eligible child, an Individualized Family Service Plan meeting must be held within 45 days of the referral. You, the EIO or designee, your service coordinator, and evaluators participate — and you may bring anyone else you want. The IFSP is written at this meeting, and services begin once you give written consent.

You are a full member of this team, not an observer being informed of a decision. Nothing in the IFSP takes effect without your written consent, and you may consent to some services and decline others.

If day 45 comes and goes with no IFSP meeting, put your concern in writing to the EIO the same week: state the referral date, the days elapsed, and ask for the meeting to be scheduled immediately. Delay is the single most common failure in this system, and a dated email is what turns a vague frustration into a documented compliance issue — and, if necessary, the basis of a state complaint.

The IFSP: Not a Small IEP

Families who have heard about IEPs from older siblings or friends often assume the IFSP is the toddler version of the same document. It is not, and the difference is the most important conceptual point in this guide.

An IEP is child-centered: it is a plan for a student's education, written by a school team, focused on the child's goals in a school setting. An IFSP is family-centered: it is a plan for supporting a family in helping their child develop, because at this age the family is the intervention. A therapist who sees your child for an hour a week is not the primary driver of change. You are — every day, in every diaper change, meal, bath, and book. The IFSP exists to make that possible.

Concretely, an IFSP contains:

  • Your child's present levels of development in each of the five functional areas, based on the evaluation
  • Your family's resources, priorities, and concerns related to your child's development (with your consent)
  • Outcomes for the child and for the family — this is the part families most often leave blank and most often regret
  • The specific services: type, frequency, intensity, duration, method, and location
  • The natural environment where each service will be delivered — and a justification if it will not be
  • Projected start dates and expected duration
  • The name of the ongoing service coordinator
  • Steps to support the transition out of Early Intervention at age three

The IFSP is reviewed at least every six months and rewritten annually. You can also request a review at any time — you do not have to wait for the six-month mark if something is not working.

What to insist gets written into your IFSP

Verbal agreements at an IFSP meeting are worth nothing three months later when the provider changes. If it matters, it goes in the document. Push for:

  • Specific frequency and duration — "speech therapy 2x per week, 45 minutes, individual" rather than "speech as needed" or "1–2x weekly."
  • The actual location — "in the home and at Little Sprouts Childcare" rather than "community."
  • Parent coaching as a named component, so the therapist's job explicitly includes teaching you strategies to use between sessions.
  • Family outcomes in your own words — "our family will be able to take Maya to the grocery store without a meltdown," "we will have a way for Eli to tell us what he wants instead of screaming." These are legitimate IFSP outcomes and they anchor the whole plan in your real life.
  • Makeup session expectations — what happens when a provider cancels, and who is responsible for rescheduling.
  • Coordination language — that providers will communicate with each other and with your child's daycare, so you are not the only information channel between five adults.

What Services Are Available

Early Intervention in New York covers a broad menu. Which of these your child receives depends on the evaluation and the IFSP team's decisions, not on a fixed package.

Speech-language therapy

The most commonly delivered EI service for autistic toddlers. Covers expressive and receptive language, play-based communication, feeding and oral-motor concerns, and augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) — picture systems, signs, and speech-generating devices. AAC does not delay speech; the evidence consistently shows it supports it. See our speech therapy guide for what good early speech therapy looks like.

Occupational therapy (OT)

Fine motor skills, self-care and feeding, play skills, and sensory processing and regulation. For many autistic toddlers this is where sensory strategies that make daily life livable get built. Our occupational therapy guide covers this in depth.

Physical therapy (PT)

Gross motor development, strength, balance, coordination, gait, and mobility equipment. Motor differences are common alongside autism and frequently underestimated.

Special instruction

Delivered by a special educator, this is developmental teaching embedded in play and routines — building cognitive, play, social, and pre-academic skills, and coaching parents to do the same. For autistic toddlers, a strong special instructor is often the most valuable person on the team.

Applied behavior analysis and behavioral services

Behavioral intervention, including ABA-based approaches, may be included in an IFSP where appropriate. At this age the strongest evidence supports naturalistic, play-based, parent-mediated models rather than long hours of adult-directed drilling. Ask any behavioral provider how they embed teaching into routines, how they involve you, and how they respond to your child's distress. Our ABA therapy guide covers the questions to ask and the concerns families and autistic adults have raised.

Family training, counseling, and home visits

Services delivered to you — instruction, coaching, and support to help your family understand your child's needs and build their development. This is an explicit, fundable EI service, not a favor.

Service coordination

Every eligible child has an ongoing service coordinator who arranges services, monitors the IFSP, convenes reviews, and coordinates the age-three transition. Service coordination is itself a service and is included at no cost.

Assistive technology devices and services

Equipment and the support to use it — communication devices, adaptive seating, switches, adapted toys — including evaluation, customization, and training for the family.

Vision and hearing services, nutrition, social work, psychological and health services

Specialized supports including services for children who are deaf, hard of hearing, blind, or have low vision; nutrition services for feeding difficulties and restricted diets (common with autism); social work and psychological services; and health services needed to allow a child to benefit from other EI services.

Transportation

Transportation and related costs necessary for the child and a parent to receive EI services, where warranted.

Natural Environments: Where Services Happen

Federal law and New York regulation require that Early Intervention services be provided in natural environments — the settings where children without disabilities spend their time. In practice, that means your home, your child's daycare or family child care, a playgroup, the park. Services may be delivered somewhere else only when the IFSP team determines that the outcome cannot be achieved satisfactorily in a natural setting, and the justification must be written into the IFSP.

This is not bureaucratic trivia. It is the mechanism by which early intervention works at all. Toddlers do not generalize well from a therapy room to a kitchen. A child who learns to request a bubble in a clinic has learned to request a bubble in a clinic. A child who learns to request during their actual bath, with their actual parent, has learned something that will still be there on Tuesday.

When you are offered clinic-only services: you can push back, and the law is on your side. A useful, non-confrontational script is: "I understand the provider prefers the office. My understanding is that services need to be in the natural environment unless the team documents why that isn't possible. Can we write the home and daycare into the IFSP, or can you put the justification for the clinic setting in writing?" Asking for the justification in writing very often resolves the question on its own — because in most cases no valid justification exists.

The same principle applies to your child's daycare. If your child attends child care, EI services can generally be delivered there. This requires coordination with the provider and the program, and it sometimes takes persistence — but "we only do home visits during the day" is a scheduling preference, not a legal standard.

Your Rights and How to Enforce Them

IDEA Part C gives parents specific, enforceable procedural safeguards. Your service coordinator must give you a written explanation of these rights. Read it, keep it, and know the four that matter most.

  • Written prior notice. Before the county proposes or refuses to start, change, or end an evaluation, an eligibility determination, or a service, it must notify you in writing, in advance, with the reasons. If a county refuses something verbally, you may ask for that refusal in writing — and you should.
  • Informed written consent — and the right to decline. No evaluation and no service happens without your written consent. You may accept some services and decline others, and declining one service cannot be used to withhold another. Consent may be withdrawn at any time.
  • Access to records and confidentiality. You may review your child's early intervention records, request copies, and ask for corrections. Your family's information is protected.
  • Mediation. A voluntary, no-cost process in which a neutral, trained mediator helps you and the county reach agreement. Mediation cannot be used to delay your right to a hearing, and a mediated agreement is written down and binding.
  • Impartial due process hearing. You may request a hearing before an impartial hearing officer to resolve disputes about identification, evaluation, eligibility, placement, or services. Early Intervention hearings are designed to move quickly — far faster than school-age special education hearings — because the child is in a narrow developmental window.
  • State complaint to the Department of Health. If you believe the county or a provider has violated Part C requirements — missed the 45-day deadline, failed to deliver an authorized service, refused written notice — you may file a written complaint with the NYS DOH Bureau of Early Intervention, which must investigate and issue findings. This is often the most practical remedy for a compliance failure, and it does not require a lawyer.
  • Interpretation and translation. Notices, meetings, and the IFSP process must be accessible in your native language or preferred mode of communication.
"We don't have a provider available" is not a legal basis for denying an authorized service. Once a service is written into your IFSP and you have consented, the Early Intervention Official is responsible for arranging its delivery. Provider shortages are real, especially in rural counties and for bilingual services — but they are the county's problem to solve, not a modification of your child's entitlement. If you hear this, respond in writing: ask what specific steps the county is taking to locate a provider, ask for that answer in writing, ask for the missed-services period to be documented, and say that you will file a state complaint if the service is not delivered. Counties can and do expand provider agreements, authorize out-of-county providers, and approve alternative arrangements when families push.

The Age-Three Cliff: Transition to CPSE

Here is the part nobody warns families about. On your child's third birthday, Early Intervention ends. It does not taper. It does not extend because things are going well. The entire structure you have spent eighteen months building — the coordinator who knows your family, the therapist who comes to your living room, the plan written around your priorities — is replaced by a different system, under a different law, run by a different agency, with a different standard for where services happen.

The good news is that the handoff is planned, and you have months of warning if you know to use them.

What changes, and when

  • Age 2 years, 3 months Transition planning is expected to begin. Your service coordinator should raise it with you around this point — and if they have not by your child's 27-month review, raise it yourself. This is also when you should start learning what your school district's preschool programs actually look like.
  • Notification to the school district With your consent, the Early Intervention Official notifies your local school district that your child may be eligible for preschool special education. This notification is what puts your child on the district's radar; without it, the district does not know your child exists.
  • At least 90 days before the third birthday A transition conference is held with you, your service coordinator, and a representative of the school district. A transition plan is written into the IFSP. Federal rules allow this conference to be held up to nine months before the third birthday — earlier is generally better, because it leaves time to evaluate.
  • Referral to the CPSE and evaluation You make a written referral to your school district's Committee on Preschool Special Education (CPSE). The CPSE arranges a new evaluation by an approved preschool evaluator — at no cost to you — because Part B eligibility is a separate determination. EI eligibility does not automatically carry over.
  • CPSE meeting and the first IEP If your child is found eligible as a "preschool student with a disability," the CPSE develops an IEP and recommends services and placement. Aim to have this done well before the birthday so services begin without a gap.
  • The third birthday Part C ends. New York has specific rules about the exact start date of preschool services depending on when in the year your child turns three, and in some circumstances a child may continue in Early Intervention until the preschool program begins. Ask your service coordinator which date applies to your child's birthday — this detail decides whether your family experiences a seamless handoff or a several-month gap in services.
The gap is the single biggest risk in this transition. Evaluations take time, CPSE meetings get scheduled weeks out, and summer complicates everything. Families who start the transition conversation at 2 years 3 months usually do fine. Families who start at 2 years 10 months frequently lose two or three months of services at exactly the age when consistency matters. Start early. Put your CPSE referral in writing and keep the dated copy.

Early Intervention vs. CPSE: what actually differs

Dimension Early Intervention (EI) Preschool Special Education (CPSE)
Governing law IDEA Part C IDEA Part B (Section 619)
Age range Birth to the third birthday Age 3 through age 5 (until eligible for kindergarten)
Who administers it NYS Department of Health, through the county Early Intervention Official Your local school district, through the Committee on Preschool Special Education
The plan IFSP — family-centered, includes family outcomes, reviewed every 6 months, rewritten annually IEP — child-centered and education-focused, reviewed at least annually
Cost to family None. No fees, no income test; insurance billed but no family copays or deductibles for EI services None. Preschool special education and evaluations are provided at no cost to parents
Where services happen Natural environments — home, daycare, community — unless the IFSP documents why not Least Restrictive Environment (LRE) — a continuum from related services in a community preschool to a special class
Family's role Central. The family is the intervention; parent coaching and family outcomes are built into the plan Important, but the frame shifts to the child as a student; parents are IEP team members
Service coordination A dedicated ongoing service coordinator is assigned to every eligible child No equivalent role. The CPSE chairperson runs meetings; day-to-day coordination falls largely to parents
Typical services Speech, OT, PT, special instruction, behavioral services, family training, assistive technology, service coordination Related services (speech, OT, PT, counseling), SEIT (Special Education Itinerant Teacher), special class in an integrated setting, special class

Two shifts deserve emphasis. First, the loss of the service coordinator catches families hard. In EI someone whose actual job is coordination answers your calls. In CPSE, that person does not exist — you become the coordinator. Build your own file system before the transition, not after.

Second, the standard for setting changes from "natural environments" to "least restrictive environment." They sound similar and are not. Natural environments presumes services come to your child's ordinary life. LRE presumes your child is educated with non-disabled peers to the maximum extent appropriate — a continuum that includes placements in specialized settings. Under LRE, more of the conversation is about where your child goes, and you will need to advocate for inclusive options rather than assume them. Our complete IEP guide covers how to prepare for that shift, and New York State autism school resources covers the district-level landscape you are entering.

Practical Tips That Actually Change Outcomes

Document everything, from day one

Start a binder or a folder on your phone the day you make the referral. Log every call: date, time, who you spoke to, what they said. Keep every evaluation report, every IFSP, every notice. The families who get good outcomes in this system are not the loudest — they are the ones who can say "on March 4 I was told X, and here is the email."

Put requests in writing — always

A phone call vanishes. An email exists. After any significant verbal conversation, send a short follow-up: "Thanks for speaking with me today. Confirming my understanding: you said the county is looking for an OT and expects to have someone within two weeks. Please correct me if I've got that wrong." This is polite, it takes ninety seconds, and it creates a record that makes deadlines real.

Bring a second person to every meeting

A partner, a friend, a grandparent, a parent advocate. One of you participates and one of you takes notes. Meetings about your own child are emotional, and you will not remember half of what was said. You have the right to bring anyone you choose to an IFSP meeting — you do not need to ask permission or explain who they are.

Never accept a verbal denial

If the answer to a request is no, ask for it in writing with the reasons — that is prior written notice, and you are entitled to it. A surprising number of informal denials evaporate when someone has to commit them to paper and sign their name.

Treat provider shortages as a solvable problem

As above: "we don't have anyone" is a starting position, not an ending. Ask what the county is doing, ask for it in writing, ask about out-of-county or telehealth options, and name the state complaint process if weeks pass without movement. Families who do this get providers; families who accept the first answer often wait months.

Ask for the coaching, not just the therapy

Your child gets perhaps two to four hours of professional time a week and 100+ waking hours with you. The highest-leverage question you can ask any EI provider is: "What should I be doing between your visits?" Ask it every session. A good provider will welcome it; a provider who cannot answer it is not doing early intervention as designed.

Keep a short video log

Thirty-second phone videos every few weeks — playing, eating, communicating, a hard moment. They are invaluable at IFSP reviews, at the CPSE evaluation, and at any diagnostic appointment, because they show what a stranger in a one-hour session cannot see. They are also, later, a record of how far your child has come.

Find other parents

No guide replaces a parent two years ahead of you who knows which agencies in your county actually return calls. New York's parent-to-parent networks and local family support organizations exist for exactly this. See our guide to parent-to-parent support in New York State.

Your first-week checklist

  • Call your county Early Intervention Program and make the referral — today if possible
  • Write down the referral date and count forward 45 days; put that date in your calendar
  • Start a file: notes, emails, reports, business cards
  • Ask your Initial Service Coordinator for their direct contact information and the written parent rights notice
  • Separately request a developmental/diagnostic evaluation through your pediatrician if you want one — the two tracks run in parallel
  • Ask whether the evaluation can happen at home or at your child's daycare
  • Write down your top three family priorities before the IFSP meeting, in your own words
  • If your child is already 2 years 3 months or older, ask about transition planning at the very first meeting

A Word to the Parent Reading This at 11 p.m.

If you are up late searching, running your child's development through your head, you are doing something right. Attention is the first intervention. But you do not have to carry this alone, and you do not need to have it all figured out before you act. You need one phone call.

The Early Intervention Program is not a judgment about your parenting or a verdict about your child's future. It is a service your family is entitled to, paid for, and designed to help you do what you are already trying to do. Children who go through it are not broken children being repaired. They are children getting the support that lets them show the world who they are sooner.
