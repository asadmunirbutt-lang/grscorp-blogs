Building Circles of Support: Neurodiversity-Affirming Relationships for Autistic People

📅 October 15, 2026 ✍️ AMB 📁 Community & Relationships

Elena has autism, intellectual disability, and limited speech. By traditional metrics, she's dependent. She needs support with daily living, she can't hold a job, and she communicates through a combination of words, AAC device, and body language.

But Elena's circle of support is remarkable. Her family coordinates with her day program, which hired Elena's neighbor from her church as her job coach. That neighbor sits with her during volunteer work twice a week and knows exactly how to help Elena succeed. Her sister manages finances but includes Elena in every decision. Her parents facilitate relationships with three close friends who visit regularly.

Elena is profoundly supported. She's also profoundly integrated into her community. She has relationships, purpose, and agency.

This is what circles of support actually look like. Not isolation in a group home. Not dependence on paid caregivers alone. Not treating autistic people as perpetual children. Real relationships with real people who understand and respect who you are.

What Are Circles of Support?

Circles of support are a framework for building genuine community around a disabled person. Instead of relying only on paid services, you intentionally create a network of relationships—family, friends, community members, professionals—all connected by shared commitment to one person's wellbeing and inclusion.

The key word is "authentic." Not paid relationships dressed up to look like friendship. Not obligatory family service. Real people choosing to be in this person's life because they care.

The Traditional Model Doesn't Work

Most disability services operate on a professional model: you hire someone to provide support. They're trained, accountable, and predictable. But there's a profound problem.

Paid care is transactional. The caregiver leaves at 5pm. They rarely stay long enough to develop real relationship. Staff turnover is constant. And there's an inherent power imbalance—the disabled person is the client, the recipient, the one who needs. They're passive, not active in the relationship.

For autistic people who have limited natural friendships, this is isolating. You get your needs met, but you don't get community. You don't get genuine relationship. You don't get to be valued as a person, only as a case to manage.

How Circles of Support Work: The Structure

Core Roles in a Circle

The Facilitator

Usually a family member or professional who coordinates the circle. They don't do all the support—they make sure everyone stays connected and the circle keeps functioning. This might be a parent, a professional, or an older sibling.

Inner Circle: Close Relationships

Family and close friends who know the person deeply and are deeply invested. These are the people who show up consistently, who get consulted on big decisions, who provide emotional support and tangible help.

Middle Circle: Regular Community

People who see the person regularly but don't provide direct care. A job coach. A program staff member. A neighbor. A peer with autism. These relationships are real, though less intimate.

Outer Circle: Service Providers & Professionals

Therapists, doctors, case managers. Important but transactional. They shouldn't be the only relationships in someone's life.

Building an Actual Circle: Practical Steps

Step 1: Define the Person, Not the Disability

Before you build a circle, understand who this person actually is. Not their diagnosis—their personality, strengths, interests, sense of humor, values. What do they enjoy? What are they good at? What makes them laugh?

An autistic person who loves trains needs different support strategies than one who loves art. Someone who's funny and socially engaged will attract different people than someone who's quiet and introspective. You're building a circle around a person, not a diagnosis.

Step 2: Map Existing Relationships

Who already cares about this person? Family, sure. But also neighbors, friends, people from church or community groups, people from school or work. Even casual relationships matter. The person who sees them at day program twice a week. A teacher who went above and beyond. A peer with similar interests.

Key insight: Don't overlook relationships that seem small. An autistic person's shallow relationships with many people are often more sustaining than neurotypical people realize. That person who greets them warmly but briefly might be deeply valued.

Step 3: Recruit Intentionally

Some people will naturally be in a circle. Others need to be invited, and some might decline. That's okay. Be specific about what you're asking:

  • "We're building a community around David. Would you be willing to have him over for dinner once a month?"
  • "Sarah really enjoys spending time with you. Could you be a point person if something comes up with her work?"
  • "We're looking for someone to help Marcus with cooking classes on Tuesday afternoons. Interested?"

People often want to help. They just need to know what's needed and that it's okay to say no.

Step 4: Communicate and Coordinate

A circle only works if people know what's going on and feel connected. This might mean:

  • A simple email or group chat with circle members
  • Regular in-person meetings (quarterly circle gatherings)
  • A shared document with information everyone needs (triggers, communication preferences, medical info)
  • Clear communication about decision-making and who decides what

Neurodiversity-Affirming Circles: What's Different

Acceptance, not "fixing": The circle celebrates autistic traits instead of trying to eliminate them. Sensory preferences are accommodated, not pathologized. Special interests are welcomed, not considered obsessive. Stimming is normal, not something to suppress.

Autonomy and voice: The autistic person participates in decisions about their own life as much as possible. They're consulted on big decisions. Their preferences matter, even if they have disabilities.

Real relationships: Not training, not therapy, not case management. Actual friendship, mentorship, community participation. People spending time together because they want to.

Growth through participation: Skills develop through doing real things in real communities, not through isolated skill-training. Working alongside a mentor is more effective than therapy sessions in an office.

Who Should Be in an Autistic Person's Circle?

  • Peers with autism: Other autistic people often understand in ways neurotypical people never will. Peer relationships are powerful.
  • Mentors: Not therapists—adults who share interests or can model skills the person wants to develop.
  • Community connections: From church, hobby groups, volunteer settings. Places where the person participates alongside non-disabled people.
  • Advocates: People who will stand up for the autistic person's rights and preferences.
  • Practical support: Job coaches, support coordinators, direct support professionals who understand neurodiversity.
  • Family and close friends: Who provide emotional support and unconditional care.

What Happens to the Circle When Parents Can't Be the Facilitators?

The terrifying question for parents of disabled children: what happens after I'm gone? Circles of support provide an answer. If a circle is built intentionally, it survives the loss of any one person—even a parent.

This requires planning:

  • Developing relationships with a professional facilitator who can take over coordination
  • Building relationships with peers and community, not just relying on family
  • Training circle members on the person's needs and preferences
  • Legal structures (like supported decision-making) instead of guardianship when possible

The Power of Real Community

Circles of support are not a replacement for good services or effective therapy. But they're something the service system can't buy: genuine community membership. A place in a group. Relationships that matter. The knowledge that someone cares about you beyond their paycheck.

For autistic people, who often feel profound loneliness despite being surrounded by support staff, this makes all the difference.
