Respite Care for Autism Families: What It Is and How to Access It

Published: September 7, 2026
By: GRSCorp Team
Category: Family Support
86% of autism caregivers report significant caregiver stress — among the highest of any disability population
$60K+ Average annual out-of-pocket cost for autism-related care, not including lost wages from reduced work hours
NYS OPWDD New York State's Office for People With Developmental Disabilities — primary gateway to funded respite services

Raising an autistic child is one of the most profound and demanding journeys a family can undertake. The love is immense. So is the workload. Between therapy appointments, school meetings, behavioral support, medical care, sensory management, and the around-the-clock attention that many autistic children require, autism caregivers carry an enormous and relentless load. Studies consistently place autism caregivers among the most highly stressed parenting populations, with cortisol levels comparable to combat soldiers in some research samples.

Respite care exists to give caregivers a genuine, temporary break — time to rest, recover, attend to their own needs, focus on other family members, or simply breathe. It is not abandonment. It is not weakness. It is a critical component of sustainable caregiving, and families who access it regularly are better positioned to provide high-quality care over the long term. This guide explains what respite care is, what forms it takes, and how families in New York State can access and fund it.

What Is Respite Care?

Respite care is temporary relief care provided to the primary caregiver of a person with a disability. During respite care, a trained provider — either an individual or a program — cares for your child while you take a break. Respite can last a few hours, a full day, a weekend, or an extended stay. It can happen in your home, in a provider's home, at a day program, or in a residential facility. The unifying feature is that it gives the caregiver time away from caregiving duties without the worry of their loved one being unsupported.

Why Respite Matters — For Your Child Too

Respite care benefits caregivers obviously, but it also benefits the autistic individual. Well-rested, emotionally regulated parents are more patient, more present, and more effective in supporting their child. Additionally, respite provides the child with socialization opportunities, new relationships, novel experiences, and increased independence — all of which support development. Many autistic children thrive with respite providers who bring fresh energy and different approaches. Far from being a lesser form of care, quality respite can be genuinely enriching for the individual receiving it.

The Reality of Caregiver Burnout

Caregiver burnout is not a hypothetical risk — it is an extremely common outcome for autism families who do not have adequate support. Burnout manifests as chronic exhaustion, emotional numbness, resentment, depression, anxiety, physical health deterioration, and, in extreme cases, family breakdown. A 2009 study published in the journal Autism found that mothers of autistic children have stress hormone levels equivalent to those of families dealing with war, poverty, or natural disaster. More recent research has confirmed and expanded these findings.

The impacts of caregiver burnout extend well beyond the caregiver. Research links high parental stress in autism families to worse behavioral outcomes in autistic children. When caregivers are depleted, the quality of care declines, patience shrinks, family relationships suffer, and everyone in the household is affected. Accessing respite care is not selfish — it is one of the most direct ways to protect your child's wellbeing.

Types of Respite Care

In-Home Respite
A trained provider comes to your home and cares for your child in their familiar environment. This is often the most comfortable option for autistic children who struggle with transitions and new environments.
Out-of-Home / Host Family Respite
Your child goes to stay with a trained host family or respite provider's home for a few hours or days. Provides novelty and new relationships.
Day Programs / Drop-Off Respite
Structured daytime programs that provide care, activities, and peer interaction. Operates like a supervised day program with autism-trained staff.
Overnight / Weekend Respite
Extended care — from one night to a week — at a respite facility or residential program. Allows caregivers to take a genuine vacation or address family emergencies.
Camp-Based Respite
Autism-specific summer and weekend camps provide structured, engaging respite lasting days or weeks. Often eligible for Medicaid waiver funding.
Crisis Respite
Emergency short-term placement when a family is in crisis — caregiver hospitalization, domestic emergency, or acute behavioral crisis at home.

Finding Respite Providers in New York State

New York State has a network of funded respite services coordinated primarily through the Office for People With Developmental Disabilities (OPWDD). Eligibility for OPWDD services requires an autism diagnosis (or another qualifying developmental disability diagnosis), documentation of functional needs, and enrollment in the OPWDD system.

Steps to Access OPWDD Respite Services in NYS:
  • Step 1 — Establish eligibility: Contact your regional OPWDD office and submit an eligibility application with your child's diagnostic documentation
  • Step 2 — Get a Care Manager: Once eligible, you will be assigned or can select a Care Manager (also called a Support Broker or Coordination of Community Services) who helps navigate services
  • Step 3 — Develop a Life Plan: Work with your Care Manager to develop a Life Plan that identifies respite as a needed support
  • Step 4 — Access funding: Identify which waiver or funding stream covers respite for your child's level of need (see below)
  • Step 5 — Choose a provider: Select from OPWDD-certified respite providers in your region, or self-direct to hire an independent provider

In addition to OPWDD, the Early Intervention and CPSE (Committee on Preschool Special Education) programs include respite components for younger children. For school-age children, the Committee on Special Education (CSE) process may also identify family support needs. Our NYS autism school resources guide covers the full landscape of state services.

Medicaid Waivers and Funding Options

Many families assume respite care is entirely out-of-pocket — and while it can be, multiple funding streams are available, particularly through Medicaid, that make respite accessible regardless of family income.

Key Funding Sources for Respite in New York State:

OPWDD Home and Community Based Services (HCBS) Waiver: Covers respite for OPWDD-eligible individuals. The level of funding varies by individual need and available state budget. Types of funded respite include planned respite, camper respite, and family education and respite services.

Medicaid State Plan: Some respite-adjacent services (habilitation, behavioral support) are covered through standard Medicaid without waiver enrollment.

Family Support Services Program: OPWDD's Family Support Services (FSS) provides flexible funding for respite, recreation, and other family support needs to families not yet on the waiver. Funding is limited and allocation varies by county — contact your county's FSS coordinator.

ARCH National Respite Network: Maintains a national database of respite funding programs by state, including NYS-specific grants and emergency funds for families in crisis.

Non-profit and foundation grants: Organizations including the Doug Flutie Jr. Foundation for Autism and local autism organizations provide direct grants to families for respite costs.

What to Look for in a Respite Provider

Quality matters enormously in respite care. Your child is being placed in someone else's care, and the provider must understand autism, sensory needs, communication differences, and how to manage challenging situations safely and compassionately.

  • Autism-specific training: The provider should have documented training in autism support, not just general disability care
  • Experience with your child's profile: A provider experienced with minimally verbal children, or with children who have significant behavioral support needs, if that applies
  • Understanding of sensory needs: Ask how they would handle sensory overload, meltdowns, and refusal to eat unfamiliar foods
  • References from autism families: Ask for and call references from families with autistic children specifically
  • Background check: All respite providers working through OPWDD-certified agencies undergo background checks; for self-directed providers, conduct your own
  • A detailed intake process: Good providers want to know everything about your child before the first visit — preferences, fears, routines, communication strategies, emergency contacts
  • Willingness to do a gradual transition: The first respite visit should not be a cold handover; build up with shorter visits while you remain nearby

Self-Care for Caregivers: Using Your Respite Time Well

Many caregivers find that when they finally get respite time, they struggle to use it for genuine rest. The temptation to run errands, catch up on work, or simply worry about their child can consume the break entirely. While practical tasks may genuinely need attention, planning at least some of your respite time for actual restoration — whatever that means for you — is essential.

Making the Most of Respite Time:
  • Sleep — chronic sleep deprivation is near-universal among autism caregivers and has cascading health effects
  • Spend time with your partner, other children, or friends — relationships that sustain you
  • Engage in physical activity — exercise is one of the most powerful stress-reduction tools available
  • Pursue something that belongs only to you — a hobby, creative outlet, or interest
  • Access your own mental health support — therapy for caregivers is both warranted and valuable
  • Simply do nothing for a period — the permission to rest without an agenda is itself restorative

Caregiver support groups — both in-person and online — can also be a valuable source of connection, practical information, and emotional validation. The Autism Society of America, Autism Speaks, and local autism organizations all maintain caregiver peer support resources.
