Homeschooling Students with IEPs in New York State: Your Rights, Laws & Legal Protections

Homeschooling in New York is booming. Over the past 15 years, the number of homeschooled students has more than tripled—from about 17,000 in 2009 to nearly 54,000 today. But for families with children who have Individualized Education Plans (IEPs), the decision to homeschool raises critical questions: Do you lose special education services? What are your legal rights? What does the process actually look like?

The answer might surprise you: You don't lose your child's IEP services. New York State law protects your right to those services, whether your child attends public school or learns at home. But understanding your rights, navigating the legal requirements, and accessing the services your child needs requires knowing how the system works.

This guide walks you through everything families in New York—especially in the Capital Region—need to know about homeschooling with an IEP: the laws that protect you, the growing trend, your legal rights, and exactly how to maintain your child's special education services while homeschooling.

The Homeschooling Boom: Why Families Are Choosing It

Homeschooling in New York isn't a fringe choice anymore. It's a growing trend, and families with special needs children are part of that movement.

📊 The Numbers:
• 178% growth in homeschooling between 2013 and 2023
• From ~17,000 students (2009-10) to ~54,000 students (2025-26)
• 2nd fastest homeschooling growth nationally (after Florida)
• 8% growth just in the last 2 years
• About 1.8% of all NYS students now homeschooled
• Over 14,000 homeschooled in NYC alone

Why Families With IEP Children Choose Homeschooling

  • Flexibility: Custom pacing for children who learn differently
  • Reduced sensory overwhelm: For children sensitive to classroom environments, noise, crowds
  • Specialized focus: Ability to emphasize strengths and interests
  • Reduced bullying: One-on-one or small group instruction in safe environments
  • Hybrid approach: Parent teaches core subjects; district provides specialized services (speech, OT, counseling)
  • Faster instruction: Eliminate commute time, wait periods, transition time between classes
  • Mental health: Reduced anxiety for children with anxiety-related diagnoses

But here's the critical misconception families have: Choosing homeschooling doesn't mean giving up your child's IEP or the special education services they're entitled to. In fact, New York State law explicitly protects that right.

The Law: What New York Guarantees Your Child

The Core Protection: Chapter 217 of the Laws of 2008

New York State law is clear: Children with disabilities who are home-instructed remain eligible for special education services from their home school district. This protection comes from Chapter 217 of the Laws of 2008, which amended Education Law Section 3602-c.

Key Legal Principle:
A home-schooled child with a disability is entitled to attend public schools tuition-free AND is eligible to receive special education services from the district where they reside. These are not optional—they are required by law.

What This Means Practically

Your child has the right to:

  • Special education services specified in their IEP (speech therapy, occupational therapy, counseling, physical therapy, etc.)
  • An updated evaluation and IEP/IESP developed by the school district's Committee on Special Education (CSE)
  • Services delivered by the district (not something you have to pay for separately)
  • Continued services through age 21 (same as students in traditional schools)
  • Equal services compared to other students with disabilities in the district
  • Due process protections (the same rights as traditional public school students)

The Process: Step-by-Step How to Get IEP Services While Homeschooling

Step 1: Notify the District in Writing (Deadline: June 1)

Submit a written request to your school district superintendent requesting special education services. The standard deadline is June 1 for services starting the following school year.

Exception: If your child is newly identified as having a disability after June 1, or if you change districts mid-year, the June 1 deadline doesn't apply.

Step 2: Get an Individualized Home Instruction Plan (IHIP) Approved

The district will provide you with an IHIP form. This plan must include:

  • Your child's name, age, and grade level
  • Your curriculum, textbooks, and instructional materials
  • The name of whoever will provide instruction (you, or a hired tutor)
  • Dates for quarterly reports to the district
  • Your plan for assessments/evaluations

Important: You don't have to write an IEP yourself. The district develops the IEP/IESP for home-instructed students.

Step 3: The CSE Develops an IESP (Not an IEP)

For home-instructed students with disabilities, the Committee on Special Education develops an Individualized Education Services Program (IESP) instead of an IEP. The IESP is developed the same way and has the same contents as an IEP—it just recognizes the home-instruction setting.

Step 4: Receive Special Education Services

The district decides where and how services are delivered. This might be:

  • At a school building (most common)
  • At your home (depending on the service and district discretion)
  • At a BOCES location
  • A combination of locations

Key point: The district pays for these services. You don't.

NYC-Specific Additional Step

In New York City, you also need to provide the CSE with a Letter of Compliance issued by the Office of Homeschooling after your IHIP is reviewed and approved.

What About "Home Tutoring"? Is It Different?

Three Different Scenarios (Don't Confuse Them)

Scenario Who Teaches Who Pays for Instruction Duration IEP Services?
Family-chosen homeschooling Parent or hired tutor Family Long-term ✅ YES
District-provided interim home tutoring District teacher District Temporary (weeks/months) ✅ YES
Out-of-district placement School staff District Ongoing ✅ YES

District-Provided Home Tutoring (Shenendehowa Policy 8450 & Similar)

Some districts offer temporary home instruction while a student awaits an out-of-district placement (like BOCES or a private special needs school). This is different from family-chosen homeschooling:

  • District employs the teacher (not hired by family)
  • District pays for everything (not family)
  • Temporary only (reviewed every 4 weeks)
  • Bridges the gap while waiting for placement availability
  • IEP services included

Example: Your child is approved for a BOCES program, but there's no opening until next semester. The district provides temporary home instruction to keep your child engaged in school while you wait.

Your Legal Rights & Protections

Right #1: The Right to Services (Not Optional)

The district must provide the services listed in your child's IESP. This is mandated by law, not something the district can refuse or make optional.

Right #2: The Right to Equitable Services

Your homeschooled child with an IEP is entitled to services equitable to those provided to other students with disabilities in the district, whether they attend public school, BOCES, or private schools.

Right #3: The Right to Due Process

You have the same due process protections as families of children in traditional schools:

  • Right to participate in CSE meetings
  • Right to review and consent to evaluations
  • Right to request reevaluations
  • Right to request an IEP meeting if you disagree with placement or services
  • Right to dispute resolution (mediation, impartial hearing)

Right #4: The Right to Services Through Age 21

Like students in traditional schools, your homeschooled child continues to receive special education services through age 21.

Right #5: The Right to Flexibility

You can create a hybrid approach:

  • You teach core subjects (reading, math, science, social studies)
  • District provides mandated services (speech, OT, counseling)
  • You hire a tutor for subjects you can't teach
  • All coordinated through one IHIP

What's NOT Guaranteed (Important Limitations)

No State Financial Support for Instruction

New York provides no vouchers, education savings accounts, or stipends for homeschool families. The district pays for special education services only—not for your curriculum, materials, or instruction.

Service Location Isn't Always Home

The district decides where services are delivered. While some services might happen at home, many require a school setting (for peer interaction, equipment, etc.).

Parent-Written IEPs Aren't Accepted

You don't write the IEP/IESP yourself. The district's CSE develops it. However, your input is crucial and required at the IEP meeting.

Key Takeaways: What Every Homeschooling IEP Family Should Know

✓ You Keep the IEP Services: Choosing to homeschool does NOT mean forfeiting special education. The law protects your child's right to services.

✓ The Process Has Clear Steps: Written request → IHIP approval → CSE develops IESP → District provides services.

✓ You Have a Voice: You participate in IEP/IESP meetings, you have due process rights, and you can request changes if services aren't meeting your child's needs.

✓ You Can Do It Flexibly: Parent teaching + tutor + district services = a custom education tailored to your child's needs.

✓ There's a Deadline: June 1 is the typical deadline to request services for the following school year. Don't miss it.

✓ Get It in Writing: Always document your request and save all district communications.

Questions to Ask Your District

When you contact your district about homeschooling with an IEP:

  • "What is the written request process and who do I send it to?"
  • "When do you need my IHIP submission and what form do you use?"
  • "How soon after I submit my request will the CSE meet to develop the IESP?"
  • "Which services will be delivered at home vs. at a school building?"
  • "Who is my point of contact for questions about services?"
  • "Can I use a hybrid approach (me teaching some subjects, you providing others)?"
  • "What happens if services aren't meeting my child's needs—how do we adjust?"
