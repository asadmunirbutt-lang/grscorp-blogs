The Transition to Adulthood: Navigating the Autism Services Cliff

Published: September 13, 2026
By: GRSCorp Team
Category: Family Resources & Advocacy
Read time: 18 min

Ask parents of autistic teenagers what frightens them most, and you will hear the same answer over and over: what happens when school ends?

For roughly eighteen years, a family's life is organized around a system that is legally required to serve their child. There is a building to go to, a bus that comes, a team that meets, a plan on paper, and a phone number to call when something goes wrong. Then, on a single date, all of it stops — and nothing automatically takes its place.

This is what advocates call the services cliff. It is not a metaphor for a gradual decline. It is an accurate description of a legal change that happens overnight. And it is entirely survivable — but only for families who understand it years in advance and begin building the adult system while the school system is still holding them up.

This guide explains what the cliff actually is, what New York families specifically need to do and when, and how to assemble a genuinely good adult life on the other side of it.

~1 in 3 Autistic young adults are disconnected from both work and further education in the first two years after leaving high school
~1 in 4 Receive no disability services at all in the years immediately after school exit
Under 20% Commonly cited estimate for full-time competitive employment among autistic adults
Age 21 When entitlement to school services ends in New York — eligibility runs through the school year in which the student turns 21

Figures above are approximate and drawn from national post-school outcome research on young adults with autism. Studies differ in how they define employment and which age ranges they follow, so treat these as an indication of scale rather than exact rates.

The Services Cliff, Explained

The cliff exists because of a fundamental difference in how two systems are built. Understanding this difference is the single most useful thing a parent can learn before their child turns 16.

Under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), special education is an entitlement. If a student is eligible, the school district must provide a free appropriate public education. There is no waiting list for an IEP. If the district fails to deliver, the family has enforceable due process rights. The obligation sits with the system, not the family.

Adult disability services work the other way around. They are eligibility-based and capacity-limited. You must apply, prove you qualify, and then often wait — sometimes for years — for a service that has funding available. No agency is legally required to serve every person who qualifies, and no one will come looking for you. The obligation sits with the family to find, apply for, and keep re-applying to each separate program.

Before: The School System (to age 21)

  • Entitlement — services are legally owed
  • One IEP coordinates everything
  • One team, one meeting, one document
  • Transportation usually provided
  • Due process rights and mediation available
  • Services follow the student automatically each year
  • No cost to the family

After: The Adult System (age 21+)

  • Eligibility-based — you must qualify and apply
  • Multiple agencies, none of which talk to each other by default
  • Separate applications for each program
  • Transportation is often the family's problem
  • Appeals exist but are narrower and slower
  • Waitlists are normal, not exceptional
  • Funding depends on Medicaid and program budgets
The most important sentence in this article: the adult system cannot be assembled on graduation day. Determinations, waitlists, and service authorizations run on timelines measured in months and years. A family that begins at 21 will spend the first years of adulthood waiting. A family that begins at 14 or 15 walks off the cliff onto a platform they already built.

When exactly does school end in New York?

In New York State, a student with a disability remains eligible for special education services through the end of the school year in which they turn 21, or until they earn a regular high school diploma — whichever comes first. A student who turns 21 in October, for example, generally finishes that school year rather than exiting on their birthday.

That second clause deserves attention. Earning a regular diploma ends the entitlement. For some students this is exactly right — they are ready, and the diploma opens doors. For others, families and teams choose to keep the student enrolled past the traditional senior year to use the 18–21 window for community-based instruction, job sampling, travel training, and independent living skills. Neither choice is universally correct, and it deserves a real conversation with the CSE well before senior year rather than a default decision made by inertia.

Ask this at the IEP meeting: "If my child earns a regular diploma this June, what services end that day, and what would we gain by using the 18–21 years instead?" A team that cannot answer specifically has not done transition planning.

Transition Planning Timeline by Age

Transition planning is not a single event. It is a decade-long sequence in which each step depends on the ones before it. Here is what to do, and when.

Age 12–14

Build self-advocacy, and bring the student into the room

Long before any paperwork, the foundation is the young person's own understanding of themselves. This is the age to start teaching the skills that adult systems will assume they have.

  • Begin honest, developmentally appropriate conversations about the autism diagnosis — what it means, what it does not mean, and what supports help.
  • Start inviting the student to at least part of their IEP meeting, even if they only attend for ten minutes or contribute one goal.
  • Practice the vocabulary of needs: "I need a quiet space," "Can you repeat that," "I understand better when it's written down."
  • Give real responsibility at home — managing a small allowance, a chore with a deadline, an alarm clock they set themselves.
  • Start a records file. Every evaluation, IEP, psychological report, and medical letter from this point forward should be kept. You will need childhood documentation years from now for OPWDD eligibility.
Age 15

New York requires transition planning in the IEP

Federal IDEA requires transition services to be in effect by the IEP that will be in place when the student turns 16. New York starts earlier — transition planning must be included in the IEP beginning at age 15. This is one of the few places where NY families get a head start, and many do not realize they have it.

  • The IEP must include measurable postsecondary goals in education/training, employment, and (where appropriate) independent living.
  • Goals must be based on age-appropriate transition assessments — ask to see them, and ask what the student's own stated preferences were.
  • Transition services must be a coordinated set of activities, not a single line item. Course of study, community experiences, and adult living objectives all belong here.
  • The student must be invited to the IEP meeting when transition is discussed.
  • With parental consent, outside adult agencies likely to provide or pay for services can be invited to the meeting. Use this — getting an adult agency in the room while the school is still responsible is enormously valuable.
Age 16–17

Start the adult applications — especially OPWDD

This is the critical window. The applications begun here are the ones that will be finished in time.

  • Begin the OPWDD eligibility process. Do not wait. Determination and the subsequent service authorization take a long time, and documentation gathering can take months on its own. See the OPWDD section below.
  • Apply to ACCES-VR (Adult Career and Continuing Education Services — Vocational Rehabilitation). Students can typically apply while still in high school; the school transition team can help make the referral.
  • Request formal vocational assessments through the school — interest inventories, situational assessments, work sampling.
  • Pursue real work experience: paid jobs, volunteering, internships, school-based enterprises. Research consistently finds that paid work experience during high school is one of the strongest predictors of employment after it.
  • Begin travel training if public transportation will be part of adult life.
  • Tour adult day programs, supported employment providers, and residential settings now, while you have time to be choosy.
Age 17

Decide on decision-making support BEFORE the 18th birthday

Whatever you choose, choose it deliberately and early. Guardianship petitions take time to prepare and be heard; a power of attorney and health care proxy require the young person to sign while they have capacity to understand the documents.

  • Meet with a special needs attorney or a disability rights organization to review the full range of options — not just guardianship.
  • If you pursue guardianship, start the Article 17-A petition well before the birthday; courts have their own schedules.
  • If you pursue less restrictive routes, prepare the power of attorney, health care proxy, HIPAA release, and FERPA release for signing at 18.
  • Selective Service: individuals assigned male at birth are generally required to register within a window around their 18th birthday, and disability does not by itself create an exemption. Registration can affect eligibility for some federal student aid and job programs. Check current requirements at sss.gov.
Age 18

Legal adulthood arrives — with or without a plan

On the 18th birthday, several things change at once, automatically, whether or not anyone in the family is ready.

  • Educational rights transfer to the student. Unless a guardian has been appointed or another legal arrangement is in place, the student — not the parent — now consents to evaluations, signs the IEP, and controls their educational records. Schools must notify families of this transfer in advance.
  • Medical decision-making transfers too. Without a health care proxy or guardianship, a physician is not obligated to discuss care with a parent.
  • Apply for SSI as an adult. This is significant: at 18, parental income and resources are no longer "deemed" to the applicant. Many young people who were denied SSI as children because the household earned too much now qualify on their own. If your child was already receiving SSI, expect an age-18 redetermination under adult disability criteria.
  • Open or fund an ABLE account if you have not already — see our financial planning guide.
  • Register to vote if the young person wishes to. Having a disability does not remove the right to vote.
Age 18–21

Use the bonus years deliberately

For students who have not accepted a regular diploma, these years are a gift — but only if they are spent on skills that transfer to adult life rather than on repeating academic content.

  • Push for community-based instruction: shopping, banking, transit, appointments, workplace routines in real settings.
  • Look into transition or 18–21 programs, which may be district-run, BOCES-based, or hosted on a college campus.
  • Explore college-based transition and inclusive higher education options — see our autism and college guide.
  • Keep the ACCES-VR case active and move toward a signed Individualized Plan for Employment.
  • If OPWDD eligibility has been established, work with a Care Coordination Organization to build the Life Plan now, so services can begin the month school ends rather than a year later.
  • Practice the gap: try a summer or school break with the adult schedule you expect, and find out what breaks before it matters.
Age 21

The entitlement ends

School services stop. Adult services must already be authorized and starting, not applied for. By this date a well-prepared family has: an OPWDD eligibility determination, an assigned care manager and Life Plan, at least one funded day or employment service, a decision-making arrangement, SSI and Medicaid in place, and a realistic transportation plan.

If any of those are missing on the last day of school, the family absorbs the gap — usually through a parent reducing work hours or leaving a job. That is the real cost of the cliff, and it is the thing early planning prevents.

OPWDD Eligibility in New York

The New York State Office for People With Developmental Disabilities (OPWDD) is the agency that funds and oversees most long-term adult supports for people with developmental disabilities, including autism. Day services, community habilitation, residential settings, respite, self-directed budgets, and care coordination all flow through OPWDD eligibility. For many families it is the single most consequential door in the entire adult system.

The Front Door

OPWDD's intake process is known as the Front Door. Families contact their regional OPWDD office (a Developmental Disabilities Regional Office, or DDRO), attend an information session, submit documentation, and go through an eligibility determination. If found eligible, the person is connected to a Care Coordination Organization (CCO), which assigns a care manager who develops a Life Plan and helps request specific services.

Eligibility and service authorization are two separate steps. Being found eligible does not mean services start — it means you can now ask for them. Budget your timeline for both stages.

What OPWDD requires you to prove

To be found eligible, documentation generally must establish that the person has a developmental disability that:

  • Originated before age 22 — the condition must be documented as having begun during the developmental period
  • Is expected to continue indefinitely — it is a lifelong condition, not a temporary one
  • Results in substantial functional limitations in major life activities such as self-care, communication, learning, mobility, self-direction, independent living, or economic self-sufficiency
  • Is attributable to a qualifying condition, including autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, cerebral palsy, epilepsy, neurological impairment, or familial dysautonomia

Two things surprise families here. First, an autism diagnosis by itself is not automatically sufficient — the file must also demonstrate substantial functional limitation, which means adaptive functioning evidence matters as much as the diagnostic label. Second, the file must reach backward in time. A brand-new evaluation of a 20-year-old, with nothing behind it, is a weak application no matter how thorough it is.

Start this years before you need it. The OPWDD determination process and the service authorization that follows both take substantial time, and the most common reason applications are denied is not that the person did not qualify — it is that childhood documentation was missing. Psychological evaluations, adaptive behavior assessments, school records, and medical reports from earlier years are what establish onset before age 22. Those records get harder to obtain every year: providers retire, practices close, schools purge files on their own retention schedules. Request and file them while they still exist. Beginning the process at 16 or 17 is not early — it is on time.

What to gather before you apply

  • Psychological evaluation including cognitive testing, ideally with results from more than one point in time
  • A standardized adaptive behavior assessment (such as the Vineland) — this is frequently the document that carries the application
  • A diagnostic report confirming autism spectrum disorder, with the diagnosing clinician's credentials
  • Complete school records: IEPs, evaluations, CSE meeting minutes, progress reports from elementary years forward
  • Medical records documenting the condition and its history
  • A social history describing daily functioning in concrete terms — what support the person actually needs to get through a day
  • Proof of identity, residency, Social Security number, and Medicaid status
If you are denied, appeal. A denial is not the end of the process. OPWDD denials can be appealed, and many are resolved by submitting additional documentation that fills the specific gap the reviewer identified. Read the denial letter carefully — it usually names what was missing. Ask your CCO, a disability advocate, or a special needs attorney for help building the appeal. Do not restart from scratch when a targeted supplement is what is needed.

The Adult Services Landscape

Once eligibility is established, the vocabulary changes entirely. Here are the services families most often encounter, in plain terms.

Care Coordination (CCO / Health Home)

Your care manager is the closest thing the adult system has to a CSE chairperson. They develop and maintain the Life Plan, coordinate across providers, help request and renew services, and are your first call when something is not working. The relationship matters — if a care manager is unresponsive, you can request a different one. Many families do.

Day Habilitation

Structured daytime programming focused on skill building, community participation, health, and social connection. It may be site-based, community-based, or a mix. Quality varies enormously between providers, so visit during program hours and watch what participants are actually doing — not what the brochure says they do.

Community Habilitation ("Com Hab")

One-to-one support delivered in the person's own home and community rather than at a program site, working on individualized goals — cooking, budgeting, transit, social skills, hygiene, community access. For many autistic adults who find group settings overwhelming, Com Hab is a better fit than day hab, and it can be combined with work or school.

Supported Employment (SEMP)

Ongoing support to find and keep a job in the community, including job development, on-site job coaching, and long-term follow-along after ACCES-VR involvement ends. The follow-along piece is what makes it durable: many autistic adults can do the job well but need occasional help navigating a supervisor change, a schedule shift, or a social conflict.

Prevocational Services

Skill building aimed at preparing a person for future employment — work behaviors, endurance, task sequencing, workplace social norms. Prevocational services are intended as a bridge toward competitive integrated employment, not as a permanent destination. Ask any provider how many of their participants have moved into community jobs, and when.

Respite

Short-term relief for families, delivered in the home, in the community, or at a site, from a few hours to an overnight stay. Respite is frequently underused because families feel they should not need it. They should. Caregiver burnout is one of the leading causes of placement crises — see our respite care guide.

Self-Direction

Rather than receiving services from an agency's menu, the person (with a circle of support and a broker) is allocated an individualized budget and decides how to spend it within program rules — hiring their own staff, purchasing community classes, designing a personalized schedule. For families who want control and creativity, Self-Direction is transformative. It also involves real administrative work: hiring, timesheets, budget management, and annual renewals. Some families thrive on it; others prefer an agency to carry that load. Both are legitimate choices.

Employment Pathways

The default assumption for every autistic young adult should be that employment is possible. What varies is the kind of job, the number of hours, and the amount of support — not whether work is on the table at all.

ACCES-VR (Vocational Rehabilitation)

New York's vocational rehabilitation agency helps people with disabilities prepare for, obtain, and keep employment. Services can include vocational counseling, assessments, training, assistive technology, job placement, and sometimes support for college or certificate programs tied to an employment goal. The process runs through an Individualized Plan for Employment (IPE) developed with a counselor.

Two practical notes: ACCES-VR is generally time-limited and goal-directed rather than open-ended, and its services are meant to hand off to long-term supports (such as OPWDD supported employment) once a job is stable. Apply while still in high school so the case is open when it is needed.

Supported Employment and Job Coaching

A job coach helps with learning tasks, building routines, communicating with supervisors, and troubleshooting. Coaching typically starts intensive and fades as competence grows, with follow-along support remaining available. The best coaching is invisible to coworkers and focused on making the employee independent rather than permanently accompanied.

Customized Employment

Instead of fitting a person into an existing job description, customized employment starts from a detailed discovery of the person's strengths, interests, and conditions for success, and then negotiates a role with an employer — often by carving specific tasks out of several existing positions into one job that genuinely needs doing. This approach can work well for people whose skills are real but uneven, which describes a great many autistic adults.

Internships and Project SEARCH-Style Models

Business-hosted transition programs place a cohort of students at a single large employer — often a hospital, university, or government agency — for a year of rotating unpaid internships with on-site instruction, aiming toward competitive employment at the end. Similar models operate under various names. Ask your district's transition coordinator and your ACCES-VR counselor what exists in your region; availability is highly local.

Competitive Integrated Employment: the goal

Competitive integrated employment means a real job, in a typical workplace, alongside non-disabled coworkers, earning at least minimum wage and the same rate as others doing similar work. Federal and state policy have moved firmly toward making this the expected outcome rather than an exceptional one. Segregated and subminimum-wage settings have been steadily narrowed. When evaluating any program, ask directly: does this lead to a real job in the community, and on what timeline?

The ADA at work

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, employers with 15 or more employees must provide reasonable accommodations to qualified employees with disabilities unless doing so would cause undue hardship. Accommodations that frequently help autistic employees include written rather than verbal instructions, noise-reducing headphones or a quieter workspace, adjusted lighting, a predictable schedule, advance notice of changes, a single clear point of contact, and written performance expectations.

Two things worth knowing: the employee generally must request the accommodation — the employer is not required to guess — and disclosure of a diagnosis is a personal decision with real tradeoffs. Our employment guide works through disclosure timing and how to ask for accommodations in detail.

Housing Options

Housing is where the gap between what families want and what is funded is widest, and where waitlists are longest. Start these conversations years early — and start them with the young adult, whose preferences about where and with whom they live deserve the same weight anyone else's would.

Option Support level Typical funding Waitlist reality Independence
Living with family Whatever the family provides, plus in-home services like Com Hab and respite Family resources; OPWDD in-home supports; SSI contributes to household costs None — available immediately Varies widely; can be high with deliberate effort, or low by default
Supported apartment / supportive housing Scheduled staff visits rather than around-the-clock presence OPWDD supportive housing programs; Section 8 and other rental subsidies; SSI Often long; supply is limited and concentrated in certain regions High — own lease or apartment, own routines
Certified residential setting (IRA / group home) Staffed; supervised IRAs provide 24-hour support, supportive IRAs less Medicaid through OPWDD; the person contributes most of their SSI toward room and board Frequently long, and placement often depends on documented need and vacancy timing Lower — shared home, shared staff, program schedules
Shared living / host family Live-in support from a screened individual or family paid to provide it Medicaid through OPWDD (sometimes called Family Care or shared living models) Depends entirely on finding a well-matched host; the match is the bottleneck, not a queue Moderate to high — family-scale home, more flexibility than a group setting
Fully independent living Minimal or none; possibly periodic check-ins or remote supports The person's own earnings, SSI/SSDI, housing subsidies Limited by the housing market and income, not by a disability waitlist Highest
Get on lists before you need them. Housing is the service where "we'll deal with it when the time comes" fails most badly. Discuss residential options with your care manager early, understand how your region prioritizes requests, and know that most families use a sequence — living at home with growing in-home supports, then a supported setting — rather than one permanent answer chosen at 21.

Legal Decision-Making: A Fair Comparison

At 18, the law presumes the young adult makes their own decisions. Families facing that moment are often told, sometimes by well-meaning professionals, that guardianship is simply what you do. It is not. Guardianship is one option on a spectrum, and it is the most restrictive end of that spectrum.

The honest framing is this: most autistic adults need decision support, not decision removal. The right question is not "can my child make every decision perfectly?" — very few adults could pass that test — but "what specific decisions are genuinely unsafe without help, and what is the least restrictive way to provide that help?"

Arrangement What it does Rights retained Best suited for
Full guardianship (Article 17-A in NY) Court appoints a guardian with broad authority over personal and/or property decisions. NY's Article 17-A is specific to people with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Fewest — many legal rights transfer to the guardian Situations where the person genuinely cannot make or communicate decisions even with substantial support, and no lesser alternative protects them
Limited guardianship Court grants authority over only specified domains (for example, medical decisions) while the person retains the rest. Most rights outside the named domains A narrow, identifiable area of real risk with capacity intact elsewhere
Power of attorney The adult voluntarily authorizes someone to act on financial and legal matters. No court involved; revocable. All — the person keeps full legal capacity Adults who can understand and sign the document and want help with money or paperwork
Health care proxy The adult names an agent to make medical decisions if they become unable to. Pairs naturally with a HIPAA release. All — the agent acts only when the person cannot Nearly every adult, disabled or not; this is standard good practice
Representative payee Social Security appoints someone to receive and manage SSI/SSDI benefits on the person's behalf. Applies only to those benefits. All others — it touches benefit funds only People who need help managing benefit money but not other decisions
Supported decision-making (SDM) The adult keeps legal decision-making authority and formally designates trusted supporters to help gather information, weigh options, and communicate choices. New York recognizes SDM agreements. All — capacity is retained in full The large middle group: adults who can decide with help understanding, processing, and communicating
Guardianship should not be the automatic default. It is a serious legal action that removes rights an adult would otherwise hold — including, depending on scope, the right to decide where to live, whom to see, what medical care to accept, and how to spend money. Courts increasingly expect petitioners to show that less restrictive alternatives were considered. Guardianship is also not permanent by nature: it can be modified or terminated, though doing so requires going back to court. Before filing, ask an attorney what exactly you are trying to prevent, and whether a proxy, a power of attorney, a representative payee, or an SDM agreement would prevent it just as well.

A useful exercise before deciding

List the ten decisions that worry you most — a medical procedure, a lease, a large purchase, a relationship, a job offer. For each one, ask: with the right person explaining it in the right way, could they make this decision? Most families discover the list of decisions requiring true substitution is much shorter than they assumed, and that what is really needed is a reliable person in the room, not legal authority over the person.

Benefits at 18

The financial picture changes substantially at 18. Our complete financial planning guide covers ABLE accounts, special needs trusts, and estate planning in depth; here is what specifically belongs on the transition timeline.

SSI and the age-18 redetermination

Children who receive SSI are reviewed at 18 under the adult disability standard, which asks a different question than the child standard: whether the person can engage in substantial gainful activity. Some young people who qualified as children do not meet the adult test, and some who were denied as children now qualify — because parental income and resources are no longer counted once the applicant turns 18.

That second point is the one families most often miss. If your child was denied SSI because your household income was too high, apply again at 18. Prepare for the redetermination the way you would an evaluation: gather current medical documentation, functional descriptions of what a typical day actually requires, and statements from people who provide daily support.

Medicaid

Medicaid is the funding engine behind nearly all OPWDD services, which makes maintaining it non-negotiable. In most cases SSI eligibility brings Medicaid with it. If SSI is denied or lost, there are other Medicaid pathways — including programs for working people with disabilities — so a denial should trigger a conversation with a benefits counselor rather than an assumption that coverage is gone.

ABLE accounts

An ABLE account lets an eligible person save for disability-related expenses without those savings counting against SSI's resource limit in the way ordinary savings would. For a young adult starting work, an ABLE account is the practical answer to "where does the paycheck go without breaking benefits?" Contribution limits and balance thresholds are set by federal rules and adjust over time — verify current figures before relying on a specific number.

Working while keeping benefits

The single most damaging myth in this field is that taking a job means losing everything. It does not work that way.

  • SSI reduces gradually, not all at once. Earned income is subject to exclusions before it counts at all, and beyond that the benefit is reduced by a fraction of earnings rather than dollar for dollar. Working almost always leaves a person with more total income than not working.
  • Students may have additional protections. The Student Earned Income Exclusion allows younger workers who are regularly attending school to exclude a further amount of earnings.
  • Medicaid can often be retained while working. Federal provisions and state programs for working people with disabilities are designed specifically to keep health coverage in place as earnings rise — this is usually the coverage families are most afraid of losing, and usually the one best protected.
  • Work incentives counseling is free. Benefits counselors can model exactly what a given job would do to a given person's benefits. Ask ACCES-VR or your care manager for a referral before turning down a job out of fear.

Specific dollar amounts, exclusions, and thresholds change annually. Confirm current figures with SSA or a benefits counselor rather than relying on numbers from an article or a well-meaning acquaintance.

A Practical Parent Checklist

If you do nothing else from this article, do these things, roughly in this order.

  • Age 12–14: Start a permanent records file and never throw anything away — evaluations, IEPs, medical reports
  • Age 12–14: Bring your child into their own IEP meetings, even briefly
  • Age 12–14: Begin teaching self-advocacy language and give real household responsibility
  • Age 15: Confirm transition planning is in the IEP, with measurable postsecondary goals in education, employment, and independent living
  • Age 15: Ask to see the transition assessments the goals are based on, and what your child said they wanted
  • Age 16: Contact the OPWDD Front Door and begin the eligibility process — this is the deadline that matters most
  • Age 16: Request every record you will need for that application while it still exists
  • Age 16–17: Apply to ACCES-VR with help from the school transition team
  • Age 16–17: Get your teen real paid work experience, even a few hours a week
  • Age 16–17: Tour day programs, employment providers, and residential options while you have time to compare
  • Age 17: Meet a special needs attorney about decision-making — review all options, not just guardianship
  • Age 17: Prepare the documents that need signing at 18 (POA, health care proxy, HIPAA and FERPA releases), or file the guardianship petition
  • Age 17: Check Selective Service registration requirements if applicable
  • Age 18: Apply for SSI as an adult — parental income no longer counts
  • Age 18: Confirm Medicaid, and open or fund an ABLE account
  • Age 18: Understand that educational rights have transferred, and make sure the paperwork reflects your actual arrangement
  • Age 18–21: Push the IEP toward community-based instruction, travel training, and real work
  • Age 18–21: Build the OPWDD Life Plan with your CCO so services start the month school ends
  • Age 18–21: Get a benefits counseling session before your young adult starts working
  • Age 20: Confirm in writing exactly which services are authorized to begin after the last day of school, and on what date
  • Age 21: Keep advocating — the adult system rewards families who follow up, and forgets the ones who do not

What Good Looks Like on the Other Side

It is easy to read an article like this and come away with dread. So it is worth saying plainly: the cliff is a systems problem, not a verdict on your child.

Autistic adults build good lives — with jobs that use what they are actually good at, homes where the sensory environment is under their own control, friendships and relationships and routines that belong to them. The families who get there are rarely the ones with the most resources. They are the ones who started early, kept every piece of paper, asked uncomfortable questions at IEP meetings, refused to accept "that's just how it is," and treated their young adult as a person with preferences rather than a case to be managed.

You have more time than you think, and less than you would like. Use it.
