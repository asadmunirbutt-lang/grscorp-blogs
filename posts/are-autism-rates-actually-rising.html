Are Autism Rates Actually Rising? What the 2026 Research Says

Published: September 30, 2026
By: GRSCorp
Category: Research & Prevalence
Read time: 11 min

If you have an autistic child, you have probably been handed a frightening number at some point. Rates are soaring. One in thirty-one. Something must be causing it.

Two large studies published in 2026 looked hard at that claim. Both reached the same conclusion, and it is not the one that generates headlines: the number of children who are autistic has not been climbing. The number we find has.

This distinction sounds academic. It is not. It changes what you should worry about, what you should ignore, and how you read the next alarming statistic that crosses your feed.

The short version

Autism prevalence — the share of children identified as autistic — has risen sharply over decades. Autism incidence, the rate at which new cases actually occur, appears to have stayed flat. We are getting better at recognising something that was already there.

What the JAMA Pediatrics study did

The central study followed 62,081 children across 12 consecutive birth cohorts, covering children born from 1998 to 2010. Rather than counting how many children carry a diagnosis at one moment, the researchers tracked how many were newly diagnosed by age seven in each birth year — the cumulative incidence.

If autism were genuinely becoming more common, that figure should climb steadily from the 1998 cohort to the 2010 cohort. It did not.

  • Seven-year cumulative incidence ranged from 1.9% to 3.2%, median 2.4%
  • Prevalence ranged from 2.0% to 3.4%
  • Across twelve consecutive birth years, no statistically significant increase or decrease

Twelve years of children, born through a period when public alarm about autism rates was at its loudest, and the rate of new cases did not move.

The Danish study explains the mechanism

A second 2026 study asked a different question: not how many children are diagnosed, but which children.

Comparing children diagnosed with autism or ADHD today against those diagnosed a decade earlier, researchers found that children receiving a diagnosis now more closely resemble their peers in the general population than earlier cohorts did.

That is what a widening net looks like. A decade ago, a diagnosis typically required difficulties severe enough to be impossible to miss. Today the threshold catches children whose difficulties are real but subtler — the ones who would previously have been called shy, or difficult, or gifted-but-odd, and sent on their way without support.

What actually changed

Diagnostic criteria. The 2013 move to DSM-5 folded Asperger's syndrome and PDD-NOS into a single spectrum, which mechanically increased the number of people counted as autistic.

Who gets looked at. Girls, adults, and autistic people with average or high IQ were systematically missed for decades. They are now being identified — often late, often after years of being told nothing was wrong.

Awareness and access. Paediatricians screen more consistently. Parents know the signs. Schools refer.

Services attached to a label. When a diagnosis unlocks support, more families seek one. That is a rational response to how the system is built, not inflation.

What this does not settle

Three honest caveats, because overstating this is as wrong as overstating the epidemic.

"Flat" is not "zero change ever." These studies cover specific populations and specific birth years. They are strong evidence against a dramatic recent surge in occurrence. They are not a claim that autism rates have been fixed throughout history.

It does not mean genetics and environment are irrelevant. Autism has substantial genetic contributions and researchers continue to investigate environmental factors. Finding that incidence is stable does not mean there is nothing left to learn about causes.

It does not mean everyone who needs a diagnosis has one. The opposite: median age at diagnosis still exceeds four years, well past the point at which autism can be reliably identified. Adults, women and girls, and children of colour remain underdiagnosed. A flat incidence rate alongside rising detection means we are catching up, not that we have caught up.

Why the distinction matters to you

It removes a question that eats parents alive. "What did I do?" is the question behind the epidemic framing — something must have changed, something must be causing it, and the search for a cause very quickly becomes a search for blame. If occurrence has been stable while detection improved, the premise of that question falls away.

It protects you from people selling solutions to an epidemic. The epidemic framing is commercially useful. It sells supplements, detoxes, restrictive diets, and expensive unproven treatments. A manufactured crisis needs a cure, and someone is always ready to provide one.

It points at the real problem. If the number of autistic children is not exploding but identification is improving, the pressure on the system is not an epidemic of autism. It is an epidemic of unmet need — waiting lists, service gaps, and a workforce that never scaled to meet the population that was always there.

When you next see a scary number

Ask one question: is this prevalence or incidence? Prevalence tells you how many people carry a diagnosis. Incidence tells you how many new cases are occurring. Almost every alarming headline uses the first and implies the second.

What to do with this

Practically, very little changes in your week. Your child's support needs are what they are regardless of what the population statistics say.

What changes is the story you carry. Your child is not a symptom of something going wrong in the world. They are a member of a group that has always existed at roughly this size and has only recently started being counted properly.

The children diagnosed in 1998 and the children diagnosed in 2026 are, on the evidence, occurring at the same rate. The difference is that today's children are far more likely to be found — and being found early is the single biggest predictor of getting help that works.

Sources

  • Cumulative Incidence and Prevalence of Autism Spectrum Disorder, JAMA Pediatrics, 2026 — 62,081 children across 12 birth cohorts
  • Danish registry study on changing diagnostic thresholds in autism and ADHD, September 2026
  • CDC Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring Network — current prevalence estimate of 1 in 31 children
