Why Autism in Women Goes Undiagnosed for Decades: The Masking Epidemic

📅 October 7, 2026 ✍️ Asad Munir 📁 Diagnosis & Self-Discovery

I remember sitting across from a woman named Sarah in my office. She was 34 years old, a marketing manager, married with two kids. She'd come to me because her son had just been diagnosed with autism, and while reading about his diagnosis, she saw herself everywhere in the descriptions. "But nobody ever told me I was autistic," she said. "I just thought I was broken."

Sarah's story is far from unique. In fact, it's becoming the norm. Autism in women is systematically missed, and the reason isn't that women are harder to diagnose. It's that we built our entire understanding of autism around how it presents in boys.

The Problem: Autism Was Diagnosed Through a Male Lens

When autism was first described by Leo Kanner in the 1940s, he observed boys. The earliest diagnostic criteria focused on the ways autism shows up in boys—stimming that's visible, sensory meltdowns that look like tantrums, social withdrawal that's obvious to others. These became the standard against which all autism was measured.

But autistic girls were developing different coping mechanisms. They were masking.

Girls are socialized from childhood to be quiet, empathetic, and people-pleasing. An autistic girl doesn't skip recess because she's "broken"—she sits on the bench and observes other kids intensely until she learns the social script. She doesn't have a meltdown in class—she internalizes it and falls apart at home. She doesn't stim visibly—she learned to do it in socially acceptable ways, like leg bouncing under her desk.

The result? She looks normal. Functional. "Just a little shy." So nobody looks closer.

Misdiagnosis: The Default Path for Autistic Women

When autistic women finally do seek help, they rarely get an autism diagnosis first. Instead, they typically get diagnosed with:

  • Anxiety disorder (the most common misdiagnosis)
  • Depression (especially later in life, as burnout sets in)
  • ADHD (though this often exists alongside autism)
  • OCD (especially if they have rigid routines or special interests)
  • Eating disorders (often related to sensory issues with food)
  • Borderline Personality Disorder (due to intense emotional reactions and difficulty with relationships)

These are real symptoms, mind you. But they're not the root cause. They're the consequences of years of masking, burnout, and unmet needs.

The Hidden Signs: How to Spot Autism in Adult Women

If you're a woman wondering if you might be autistic, here are the signs that clinical psychology has finally started paying attention to:

Social Masking and Exhaustion

You can do social interaction, but it completely wipes you out. After a day at work with lots of interaction, you need to be alone for hours to recover. You can perform "normal," but it requires intense, constant effort. You might have a script for certain interactions that you've memorized so well that people think you're naturally outgoing.

Intense Special Interests

People think you're just "really passionate" about your interests, but the depth goes beyond normal. You have encyclopedic knowledge about one or two topics. You think about them constantly. You organize your time around them. In women, these interests are often deemed "acceptable" (reading, art, history) rather than stereotypically autistic (trains, video games), so they don't trigger alarm bells.

Sensory Sensitivity

Scratchy clothing feels unbearable. Certain sounds make you want to scream. Strong smells trigger headaches. Fluorescent lights give you migraines. You've always been "difficult" about textures and tastes. You need things organized in very specific ways.

Perfectionism and Rigid Thinking

You set impossibly high standards for yourself. You struggle with flexibility and change. When plans change, you feel derailed for hours. You think in systems and rules. You can see when things are "wrong" or illogical, and it bothers you intensely.

Late Diagnosis Often Triggered by Burnout

Many autistic women don't seek diagnosis until they hit a wall. A major life change (becoming a parent, starting a new job, divorce, loss). Years of masking finally catches up, and they experience severe burnout, depression, or anxiety that won't respond to treatment. That's when someone, often their child's diagnosis or a therapist willing to look deeper, finally suggests: "Have you ever been tested for autism?"

Why This Matters

Getting diagnosed later in life can feel devastating. Suddenly, you're grieving all the years you spent thinking you were just "broken" or "not good enough." You spent decades working twice as hard as your peers just to appear normal. You made life choices based on a false understanding of yourself.

But a late diagnosis is also profound relief. It's not that you're broken. Your brain works differently, and that's not inherently a flaw—it's just different. You're not lazy for needing quiet after social interaction. You're not ungrateful for maintaining intense routines. You're not oversensitive or dramatic about sensory experiences.

And once you know, everything changes. You can stop masking. You can design your life around your actual needs. You can find community with people who experience the world the way you do.

Getting Diagnosed as an Adult Woman

If this resonates with you, here's what you need to know:

  1. Find a clinician who has specific experience diagnosing autism in women. Many psychiatrists still use the old male-focused criteria.
  2. Bring evidence. School reports, photos from childhood, old journals. Clinicians need to assess your whole history, not just your current functioning.
  3. Be honest about masking. Explain how much effort it takes you to socialize. This is more informative than whether you can do it.
  4. Don't assume you don't fit because you have friends or are successful. Autistic women can be high-achieving and socially skilled. The question isn't "can you do it?"—it's "at what cost?"
