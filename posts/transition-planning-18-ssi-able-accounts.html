Transition Planning at 18: SSI, ABLE Accounts & Benefits You Need to Know

📅 October 15, 2026 ✍️ Asad Munir 📁 Financial Planning & Benefits

The week your autistic child turns 18, everything changes legally. You're no longer automatically their healthcare decision-maker. You can't access their medical records without permission. The school system's free services evaporate. And suddenly, you're managing a bewildering set of government benefits with Byzantine rules that were last updated in 1996.

I've watched families make mistakes at this critical juncture that cost them tens of thousands of dollars. An autistic young adult takes a job paying $15/hour and loses SSI because no one explained work incentives. A family sets up a savings account for their child's future and accidentally triggers asset limits that cut off benefits. Someone inherits money and loses Medicaid because nobody knew about ABLE accounts.

The good news: you can navigate this. The bad news: you have to navigate it alone. Nobody's going to hand you a roadmap. So let's build one together.

What Changes at 18?

Legal Authority

At 18, your child is a legal adult. You can't make decisions for them anymore unless you've gone through guardianship proceedings. Many families don't realize this until they try to sign their child into therapy and get refused.

⚠️ Guardianship is a big decision. It gives you legal authority to make decisions, but it also removes your child's autonomy and can interfere with future independence. Consider alternatives like power of attorney or supported decision-making first. Some states are phasing out guardianship because of these concerns.

School Services End

IDEA (Individuals with Disabilities Education Act) services end at age 21, but transition planning should begin at 16. Many schools are terrible at transition planning. You need to be proactive about:

  • Figuring out employment goals
  • Planning community integration and social services
  • Identifying post-secondary education or training options
  • Coordinating with adult services (like vocational rehabilitation)

Benefits Explained: SSI and How Not to Lose It

Supplemental Security Income (SSI)

What it is: Monthly payment to eligible disabled people with limited income and resources. For 2026, the maximum is approximately $943/month, though it varies by state.

Who qualifies: Autistic people with significant functional limitations and limited income/assets.

How you lose it (the part nobody tells you):

  • Excess income: If your child earns more than $1,470/month (2026), they lose SSI. But there's a catch: they can work and keep SSI through "work incentives."
  • Excess resources: If they have more than $2,000 in savings/assets, they lose SSI. Got an inheritance? Lost your benefits. Set up a college fund? Lost your benefits. This is why ABLE accounts matter.
  • Living situation changes: If they move into a group home or institution, SSI changes (usually decreases).

Work Incentives: How to Keep SSI While Working

Plan to Achieve Self-Support (PASS)

A PASS allows you to set aside income and resources for a specific work goal. Want to save $5,000 for vocational training? Put it in PASS. The money doesn't count against SSI limits. This is gold for families planning education or work preparation.

Impairment-Related Work Expenses (IRWE)

Medical and work-related costs connected to disability are deducted from income before SSI calculation. Need a job coach? That's an IRWE. Specialized transportation to work? IRWE. This effectively increases the amount you can earn while keeping SSI.

Student Earned Income Exclusion (SEIE)

If your child is still in school (up to age 22), the first $1,900/month of work income doesn't count against SSI. This was designed for students with school-to-work transitions.

The bottom line: Your child can work. Your child can earn significant money. You just need to structure it through work incentives. Get help from a Work Incentives Planning and Assistance (WIPA) project—they're free and funded by Social Security specifically to help with this.

ABLE Accounts: The Game-Changer for Savings

If you have an autistic child, ABLE accounts might be the most important financial tool you haven't heard of.

What ABLE accounts are: A special savings account for disabled people. Contributions are limited ($18,000/year for 2026), but accumulated savings don't count against SSI or Medicaid limits (up to $100,000). Once you hit $100,000, SSI pauses but restarts when it drops back below.

Why this matters: You can save for your child's future without destroying their benefits. College fund? ABLE. Down payment for a car? ABLE. Vacation money? ABLE. It's the workaround to the $2,000 asset limit that has strangled families for decades.

  • Eligible: People diagnosed as disabled before age 26
  • Contribution limit: $18,000/year (2026) from any source (family, self, anyone)
  • Asset limit before triggering SSI pause: $100,000
  • Tax-free growth: Investment earnings aren't taxed if used for qualified disability expenses

Medicaid: The Hidden Anchor

Many autistic people need Medicaid more than they need SSI. Medicaid covers therapy, specialist visits, and medications that private insurance won't. Losing Medicaid is often worse than losing SSI.

Here's what people get wrong: SSI and Medicaid are linked in many states, but not all. In some states, they're separate. If your child loses SSI through work, you might keep Medicaid. In other states, you lose both.

The work incentive that covers this: Medicaid Buy-In programs allow disabled people to work and earn above normal limits while keeping Medicaid. Check your state's specific rules.

The Transition Checklist: What to Do Now

  1. Start transition planning by age 16 (or as soon as you know they'll need ongoing support)
  2. Get free help: Contact your state's Protection & Advocacy agency or vocational rehabilitation office
  3. Open an ABLE account if eligible (before your child turns 26)
  4. Connect with a WIPA project for work incentives planning
  5. Decide on guardianship vs. alternatives with legal help
  6. Research post-secondary options: college, vocational training, employment programs
  7. Document everything: medical records, evaluation reports, functional limitations (you'll need this for benefits applications)
  8. Plan for healthcare decision-making (through supported decision-making, healthcare POA, or guardianship)

The Bigger Picture

Transition to adult services is where the system fails families most. Schools hand you a diploma (or certificate of attendance) and tell you to figure it out. Society expects your disabled child to independently navigate Byzantine government agencies. The programs that exist are often poorly coordinated and staffed by people who don't understand autism.

But this is also where smart financial planning, knowledge of work incentives, and advance preparation make the biggest difference. You can set your child up for genuine independence and financial security if you know the system.
