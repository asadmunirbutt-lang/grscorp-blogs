Autism and Co-occurring Conditions: What Every Family Needs to Know

Published: September 9, 2026
By: GRSCorp Team
Category: Health & Wellness
Read time: 12 min
70%+ of autistic people have at least one co-occurring condition
40% also have ADHD — the single most common co-occurring condition
Many Have two or more co-occurring conditions simultaneously

Autism does not travel alone. The majority of autistic people — more than 70% by most estimates — live with at least one additional medical, developmental, or psychiatric condition. Many have two, three, or more. These conditions are not caused by autism, but they share overlapping neurological and biological roots that make them far more common in autistic individuals than in the general population.

Understanding which conditions commonly co-occur with autism matters enormously. When a co-occurring condition goes unrecognized, its symptoms are often attributed to "autism behaviors," delaying effective treatment by months or years. A child whose chronic abdominal pain is driving daily meltdowns doesn't need a behavior plan — they need a gastroenterologist. A teenager whose depression looks like increased rigidity and withdrawal doesn't need more structure — they need mental health support. Getting this right starts with knowing what to look for.

Key principle: Always treat the whole person. When behaviors are escalating, new, or changing in pattern, rule out medical and psychiatric co-occurring conditions before attributing them to autism alone. A comprehensive evaluation is not a one-time event — it should be revisited throughout a person's life.

1. ADHD (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder)

~50% of autistic people also meet criteria for ADHD

ADHD is the most frequently co-occurring neurodevelopmental condition in autism. Historically, diagnostic manuals prohibited a dual diagnosis of autism and ADHD — a restriction removed in 2013 with the DSM-5. Many people who were diagnosed with only one condition years ago may benefit from re-evaluation under current criteria.

How autism and ADHD overlap — and differ

Both conditions involve difficulties with attention, executive function, and impulse control. Both can include emotional dysregulation, social challenges, and sensory sensitivities. The differences matter for treatment: ADHD's inattention tends to be inconsistent (hyperfocus on high-interest topics vs. shutdown on low-interest ones), while autism's restricted attention is often tied to specific special interests. Hyperactivity in ADHD is typically a drive for stimulation; repetitive movement in autism is more often sensory regulation or comfort.

Why dual diagnosis matters: ADHD-specific interventions — structured task-breaking, frequent breaks, immediate feedback loops, organizational tools — are different from autism-specific supports. When only one diagnosis is treated, the other's symptoms remain unaddressed. A child struggling with distractibility and impulse control may need ADHD strategies even if their autism supports are well-established.

Treatment approaches: Behavioral therapy and parent training are first-line treatments for ADHD in young children, and these work alongside autism-informed supports. Stimulant medications (methylphenidate, amphetamine salts) and non-stimulants (atomoxetine, guanfacine) may still be appropriate for autistic people with ADHD — response rates and side-effect profiles can differ, so close monitoring with the prescribing clinician is essential. Discuss both the benefits and the potential for increased anxiety or irritability when starting any ADHD medication in an autistic patient.

2. Anxiety Disorders

~40% of autistic people have a diagnosable anxiety disorder

Anxiety is pervasive in autism — driven by sensory overload, unpredictable social demands, intolerance of uncertainty, and the daily cognitive effort of navigating a world not designed for autistic neurology. Anxiety in autism does not always look like visible nervousness. It frequently presents as meltdowns, shutdowns, increased rigidity, physical complaints, school refusal, or a spike in repetitive behaviors.

Distinguishing autism from social anxiety disorder

Social difficulties in autism stem from differences in social cognition and communication — not necessarily from fear of negative evaluation. Social anxiety disorder involves intense dread of scrutiny and judgment. Many autistic people have both: they find social interaction cognitively difficult and are afraid of it. Treating the anxiety component directly, rather than assuming all social avoidance is "just autism," can meaningfully improve quality of life.

Evidence-based treatment: Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) adapted for autism — using concrete language, visual supports, and a focus on behavioral outcomes rather than introspective reporting — has the strongest evidence base. Sensory-informed approaches that reduce the ambient anxiety load (quiet spaces, predictable routines, advance notice of changes) are also essential. For more detail, see our dedicated post on autism and anxiety.

3. Epilepsy and Seizure Disorders

25–30% of autistic people develop epilepsy at some point in their lives

Epilepsy is significantly more common in autism than in the general population (where lifetime prevalence is about 1–3%). The co-occurrence is highest in autistic people who also have an intellectual disability and in those with certain genetic syndromes such as Tuberous Sclerosis, Rett syndrome, or Angelman syndrome. Two peaks of seizure onset are well-documented: early childhood (under 5 years) and adolescence.

Why autism and epilepsy co-occur

Autism and epilepsy share overlapping neural pathways. Both involve altered excitation-inhibition balance in the developing brain, particularly in circuits involving the cortex and limbic system. Genetic variants that increase autism risk (such as mutations in SHANK3, SCN1A, or CNTNAP2) also elevate epilepsy risk. The relationship is bidirectional in some cases — uncontrolled seizure activity may itself affect neurodevelopment, compounding developmental challenges.

Types of seizures to be aware of: Not all seizures involve convulsions. Absence seizures (brief staring episodes, often mistaken for "zoning out"), focal seizures (unusual repetitive movements, emotions, or sensory experiences), and tonic-clonic (grand mal) seizures can all occur. In autistic individuals with communication differences, seizure activity may be harder to identify — behavioral changes, regression, or unexplained periods of unresponsiveness deserve investigation.

Seizure first aid (for tonic-clonic seizures): Keep calm. Do not restrain the person or put anything in their mouth. Gently turn them onto their side to protect the airway. Time the seizure. Call 911 if it lasts more than 5 minutes, if there is no recovery, or if this is their first known seizure. Afterward, stay with them and speak calmly — the postictal period (after a seizure) can involve confusion, agitation, or extreme fatigue lasting minutes to hours.

Anticonvulsant medications (such as valproate, levetiracetam, or lamotrigine) are the mainstay of epilepsy treatment. Choice of medication depends on seizure type, age, other health conditions, and potential interactions. Regular EEG monitoring and a neurologist experienced with both autism and epilepsy are essential members of the care team.

4. Gastrointestinal (GI) Issues

~47% of autistic children experience chronic GI problems

Gastrointestinal problems — including chronic constipation, diarrhea, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), abdominal bloating, and functional abdominal pain — are dramatically more common in autistic children than in neurotypical peers. A landmark meta-analysis found autistic children were 4 times more likely to experience GI symptoms. These are real medical conditions, not behavioral ones.

The GI–behavior connection

Autistic individuals, especially those with limited expressive language, often cannot tell you that their stomach hurts. Instead, chronic GI pain manifests as increased irritability, self-injurious behavior, aggression, sleep disturbance, and food refusal. When these behaviors escalate without a clear environmental trigger, a GI workup should be a priority — not a behavioral intervention. Pain drives behavior. Addressing the pain often dramatically reduces the behavior.

The gut-brain axis — the bidirectional communication network between the gastrointestinal tract and the central nervous system — is an active area of autism research. Differences in gut microbiome composition have been documented in autistic populations, though causality is still being studied. Practical approaches include dietary adjustments (fiber, hydration, identifying food sensitivities), bowel management protocols, and acid-reducing medications for GERD. A pediatric gastroenterologist is the appropriate specialist when GI symptoms are chronic or severe.

For families: Track GI symptoms alongside behavioral logs. Patterns — such as increased aggression every morning before a bowel movement, or school refusal following a night of apparent discomfort — can be compelling evidence for a medical referral and help the GI team identify the problem.

5. Intellectual Disability (ID)

~31% of autistic people also have an intellectual disability

Approximately one third of autistic people also meet diagnostic criteria for intellectual disability (IQ below 70 with significant adaptive functioning challenges). This represents a meaningful subgroup with distinct service needs — but it is equally important to recognize that the majority of autistic people do not have an intellectual disability. Autism encompasses an enormous range of cognitive profiles.

Intellectual disability affects eligibility for certain supports, educational placements, and long-term services. In school, students with both autism and ID typically receive more intensive special education services and may have different post-secondary transition goals. For adults, ID alongside autism may open access to OPWDD (Office for People with Developmental Disabilities) residential and day program supports in New York State — see our NYS resources guide for details.

Important note on assessment: Intellectual disability in autism can be overdiagnosed when assessments rely heavily on verbal or timed tasks that disadvantage autistic communication styles. Comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation using assessments validated for autism — and interpreting results in context — is essential to an accurate picture of cognitive functioning.

6. Sleep Disorders

~80% of autistic children experience significant sleep problems

Sleep problems in autism are not simply children being difficult at bedtime. They reflect genuine biological and neurological differences. Autistic children have documented differences in melatonin production — including irregular secretion patterns and altered timing — that make it genuinely harder to fall asleep at conventional times. Sensory sensitivities (the feel of sheets, ambient sounds, light levels) and anxiety add further barriers. Sleep deprivation then compounds every other challenge: behavior, learning, emotional regulation, and family functioning all suffer.

Common sleep presentations in autism
  • Delayed sleep onset: Taking 1–2+ hours to fall asleep despite routine
  • Frequent night waking: Waking multiple times, sometimes for hours
  • Early morning waking: Waking at 4–5 AM and unable to return to sleep
  • Parasomnias: Night terrors, sleepwalking, or confusional arousals at higher rates
  • Sleep-disordered breathing: Obstructive sleep apnea at elevated rates, particularly in autistic individuals with hypotonia or enlarged tonsils/adenoids

Evidence-based interventions begin with sleep hygiene and behavioral approaches: consistent bedtime routines, environmental modifications (blackout curtains, white noise, weighted blankets where sensory-helpful), and gradual delay of bedtime to match the child's natural sleep drive. Melatonin supplementation has the strongest evidence base of any pharmacological approach and is widely used in autism — always discuss dosing and timing with a physician, as the dose and delivery form matter significantly. For suspected sleep apnea or parasomnias, a sleep study (polysomnography) is the appropriate next step.

For a comprehensive look at this topic, see our detailed guide on autism and sleep.

7. Depression

Depression in autism is underrecognized, particularly in adolescents and adults. It often emerges as autistic individuals encounter greater social demands, experience repeated rejection, or begin to recognize the ways their neurology sets them apart. Autistic adults have significantly elevated lifetime rates of depression compared to the general population, and depression is a major contributor to reduced quality of life and, in severe cases, suicidal ideation.

How depression can look different in autism

Autistic people may not describe sadness or low mood in conventional terms. Depression in autism may instead present as: increased rigidity and insistence on routine (which can serve as a coping mechanism when everything feels overwhelming), social withdrawal beyond the person's baseline, a marked decline in interest in their special interests, increased stimming or self-injurious behavior, more frequent meltdowns or shutdown episodes, changes in appetite and sleep, or flat affect and reduced responsiveness. These shifts from a person's own baseline — not compared to neurotypical norms — are the signal to watch for.

Risk factors to know: Autistic people who have experienced significant bullying, social isolation, or trauma are at higher risk. Autistic people who mask heavily — particularly women, girls, and late-diagnosed individuals — carry a substantially elevated risk of depression and burnout. Late diagnosis itself can trigger a grief response as people reprocess their life history. Any expression of hopelessness, self-harm, or suicidal thoughts warrants immediate clinical attention.

Treatment: Therapy — particularly CBT adapted for autistic cognition, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), and supportive counseling — is first-line. Antidepressants (SSRIs) may be appropriate for moderate to severe depression; response can vary, and close monitoring for side effects (including increased agitation or activation) is important. Reducing environmental stressors, supporting autonomy, and affirming autistic identity are not "soft" interventions — they are clinically meaningful components of depression treatment in autistic individuals.

Putting It Together: Diagnosis and Coordinated Care

The presence of multiple co-occurring conditions means that autism care cannot be siloed. Effective support requires coordinated evaluation and communication among providers — a developmental pediatrician or psychiatrist, a neurologist (if epilepsy is present), a gastroenterologist (if GI issues are present), a sleep specialist, a mental health clinician, and the educational team.

The case for comprehensive evaluations

An initial autism diagnosis is not the end of the diagnostic process — it is the beginning. As a child grows and their profile becomes clearer, additional evaluations may be warranted. Adolescents who were never evaluated for ADHD alongside their autism diagnosis may benefit from re-evaluation. Adults who are newly diagnosed with autism should be screened for anxiety, depression, and trauma history. Co-occurring conditions can emerge, worsen, or come into focus at different life stages.

When to Seek Help: Red Flags to Watch For

Any of the following changes from a person's own baseline warrant a prompt conversation with your pediatrician or specialist — not a "wait and see" approach:

  • New or significantly increased self-injurious behavior (hitting, biting, head-banging)
  • Staring episodes, unresponsive periods, or unexplained falls — possible seizure activity
  • Persistent abdominal pain, changes in bowel habits, or significant unexplained weight loss
  • Sleep that is severely disrupted on most nights for more than 2–3 weeks
  • Sudden regression in skills that had been established (language, toileting, self-care)
  • Marked withdrawal from activities or people they previously engaged with
  • Expressions of hopelessness, worthlessness, or any mention of self-harm or not wanting to live
  • Dramatic behavioral escalation without any clear environmental explanation
Questions to bring to your pediatrician:
  • Has my child been screened for ADHD? When was the last time this was reviewed?
  • Could any current behaviors be driven by pain or a medical condition rather than autism?
  • Has my child had a developmental or neuropsychological evaluation recently?
  • Should we see a neurologist given the behaviors/history we've described?
  • Is there a GI specialist you can refer us to who has experience with autistic patients?
  • What mental health supports are available and who do you recommend for autism-informed therapy?

Navigating multiple co-occurring conditions is genuinely complex, and families do not need to figure it out alone. GRS Corp provides information, advocacy resources, and community connections to help autistic individuals and their families at every stage.

You are the expert on your family member.

When something feels off — behavior that doesn't make sense, a change that doesn't fit, a gut feeling that more is going on — trust that instinct and advocate for a fuller evaluation. Co-occurring conditions are the norm, not the exception, in autism. Comprehensive, coordinated care that addresses the whole person is not a luxury. It is what effective autism support looks like.
