Autism Summer Programs in New York State: A Family Guide

Published: September 7, 2026
By: GRSCorp Team
Category: NYS Resources
Summer Slide Research shows autistic children can lose 2–3 months of skill gains during an unstructured summer — structured programs help prevent regression
ESY Extended School Year services are a legal right under IDEA for students who meet regression-recoupment criteria — ask your IEP team
$0–cost Multiple funding streams exist — school district ESY, OPWDD, Medicaid waivers — making free or low-cost programs accessible to many families

For many families raising autistic children, September arrives with a mix of relief and anxiety — relief that school is back in session, anxiety about how much ground was lost over summer. The "summer slide" is real, and for autistic children who depend on consistent routine, therapeutic support, and structured learning, an unstructured summer can mean significant regression in skills that took months or years to build.

New York State offers a range of summer programming options for autistic children and young adults — from legally mandated school-based services to specialty camps to OPWDD-funded community programs. The challenge is knowing what exists, how to access it, and how to find the right fit. This guide covers all of it.

Why Summer Matters: Regression and the Case for Structure

The research on summer regression in autistic children is consistent. Without continuation of therapeutic services and structured learning, many children with autism lose progress in:

  • Communication and language skills (articulation, AAC use, expressive language)
  • Social skills and peer interaction patterns
  • Academic skills — particularly reading and math
  • Self-regulation and emotional coping strategies
  • Daily living and adaptive skills (toileting, dressing, meal routines)
  • Behavioral patterns, particularly when home routines become more variable

This is not inevitable — it's preventable with appropriate summer programming. And importantly, it's not just about preventing loss. Summer programs that go beyond mere maintenance offer genuine enrichment: new friendships, confidence-building experiences, and opportunities for growth outside the school-year routine.

Extended School Year (ESY): Your Legal Starting Point

Extended School Year services are not summer school in the traditional sense. ESY is a federally mandated extension of a child's IEP into the summer months, provided at no cost to families, when the IEP team determines that a child is likely to experience substantial regression during a break in services that would take significantly longer than normal to recoup.

How to Qualify for ESY in NYS

In New York State, the Committee on Special Education (CSE) must consider ESY as part of every IEP process. The standard is whether the student will experience significant regression and whether that regression will require an inordinate amount of time to recoup. You do not have to wait to be told your child qualifies — you can and should request an ESY discussion at every IEP meeting. Document any regression that occurred after previous school breaks as evidence.

Important: ESY must be individually determined — it cannot be offered to all students in a program as a blanket benefit, nor can it be denied as a blanket policy. If you believe your child qualifies and the team has not offered it, ask explicitly: "Has the team considered whether [child] meets criteria for Extended School Year services?" If denied, you have the right to request mediation or a due process hearing.

ESY services mirror the IEP — they provide the specific therapies and instruction the child receives during the school year, not necessarily a full school day. A child might receive 30 minutes of speech therapy three days per week during ESY, for example.

Types of Summer Programs in New York State

Beyond ESY, New York's autism community is served by a rich but sometimes hard-to-navigate array of summer programming options.

Specialized Autism Summer Camps

Camps specifically designed for autistic campers with staff trained in autism support, sensory accommodations, structured schedules, and low camper-to-staff ratios. Programs range from day camps to overnight options. Examples include Camp Loyaltown (AHRC — Hunter, NY), Eden II Programs camps (Staten Island), and Camp Northwood (Remsen, NY) for children and teens with learning differences including autism.

Inclusive Summer Camps with Support

General camps that provide inclusion aides or "inclusion specialists" to support campers with disabilities. NYC's Camp Sunshine and many JCC camp programs offer inclusion models. The NYC Department of Parks runs Anchor program specifically for participants with disabilities at many camp sites.

Therapeutic Day Programs

Programs that combine therapeutic services (ABA, speech, OT) with recreational and social activities in a structured day-camp format. Many ABA centers offer summer programming. Bancroft, Eden II Programs, Elija School (Long Island), and many regional providers offer these.

Social Skills Summer Programs

Specifically focused on building peer interaction, conversation skills, and social understanding. Often run by outpatient clinical practices. The PEERS curriculum (UCLA model) is delivered by multiple NYS providers in summer formats.

Vocational and Transition Programs

For older teens and young adults (typically 14+) preparing for employment and adult life. OPWDD-funded providers run many of these. ACCES-VR (Adult Career and Continuing Education Services — Vocational Rehabilitation) can fund summer work experiences and skills training for eligible young adults.

Residential / Overnight Programs

For teens and young adults ready for overnight experiences, residential summer programs provide independence, socialization, and skills practice in a supported environment. Require more planning and family preparation. Camp Jened (Rock Hill, NY) and other regional programs serve this population.

Funding Summer Programs: Don't Assume It's Out of Reach

Summer programming for autistic children can be expensive, but multiple funding streams exist in New York State that families may not know about.

School District (ESY): Free, as described above. Always your first conversation.
OPWDD (Office for People With Developmental Disabilities): Individuals who are OPWDD-eligible (developmental disability diagnosis by age 22, significant functional limitations) can access a range of summer services through their OPWDD Care Manager. Day habilitation programs, community habilitation, and respite services can all support summer participation. Contact your local OPWDD Developmental Disabilities Regional Office (DDRO) or an OPWDD-enrolled voluntary agency to discuss options.
Medicaid Waiver Programs (HCBS): New York's Home and Community Based Services waiver allows eligible individuals to use Medicaid funding for community-based services including some recreational programming. Your Medicaid Service Coordinator can advise on what summer activities your plan covers.
Private Insurance: If a summer program includes therapeutic services (ABA, speech, OT), those specific service components may be billable to private insurance. Always verify with your insurer — New York's mental health parity law (Insurance Law §3221) requires coverage of autism treatment services.
Camp Scholarships and Foundations: The Autism Science Foundation, Autism Speaks (family services grants), and many regional nonprofits including GRSCorp can connect families with camp scholarship resources. Apply early — deadlines are typically in the winter or spring.

Summer Program Locations by NYS Region

New York City / Metro Area: Eden II Programs (Staten Island), AHRC programs, YAI programs, JCC inclusive camps (Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens), NYC Anchor Program (Parks Dept.), and numerous ABA-based summer programs across all five boroughs.
Long Island: Elija School (Huntington), ACLD (Albertson), Nassau and Suffolk BOCES extended year programs, St. Mary's Healthcare System for Children (Bayside) summer programming, and the Eden II / Genesis affiliate programs.
Hudson Valley / Capital Region: Wildwood Programs (Albany and surrounding counties), CAPTAIN Community Human Services, Northeast Parent & Child Society, Autism Society of the Greater Hudson Valley programming, and GRSCorp community resources in the Capital Region.
Western New York: Baker Victory Services (Buffalo), Aspire of Western New York, CP of Western New York, and BOCES programs in Erie, Monroe, and surrounding counties.
Central / Northern NY: Enable (Utica), Resource Center for Independent Living, Northern Regional Center for IDD services, and various BOCES programs across central NYS.

Questions to Ask Any Summer Program

  1. What is your staff-to-camper or staff-to-participant ratio?
  2. What training do staff receive in autism, de-escalation, and crisis prevention?
  3. How do you handle meltdowns or dysregulation events?
  4. Can my child bring their AAC device / assistive technology? How will it be supported?
  5. How do you handle sensory needs — quiet spaces, food accommodations, sensory breaks?
  6. What is your communication protocol with families during the program day?
  7. What does a typical day look like? How much structure vs. free time?
  8. Do you involve children's existing IEP goals? Will you communicate with school providers?
  9. What is your policy on inclusion of campers who are minimally verbal or have significant support needs?
  10. What happens if the placement isn't working — how do you handle that conversation?
Timing Tip: The best summer programs fill up fast — many beginning intake in January or February for summer programs. If you're reading this in the spring, start making calls now. For ESY, your IEP team must discuss it at least by the spring annual review. Don't wait until June to raise the question.

A good summer program does more than prevent regression — it can be one of the most growth-promoting experiences in an autistic child's year. Finding the right fit takes effort, but New York State has more options than most families realize. The key is knowing what questions to ask and what funding to pursue.
