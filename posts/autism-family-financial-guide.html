Financial Planning for Autism Families: ABLE Accounts, Trusts & Benefits Guide

Published: September 9, 2026
By: GRSCorp Team
Category: Family Resources & Advocacy
Read time: 13 min

Raising an autistic child is one of the most rewarding experiences imaginable. It also carries significant financial weight that most families are not prepared for. Therapies, evaluations, adaptive equipment, specialized schooling, and long-term residential or support needs add up quickly — and the financial landscape is complex, full of rules that seem designed to confuse.

The good news: there are powerful tools designed specifically to help autism families build financial security without jeopardizing essential government benefits. This guide walks through everything you need to know — from early-childhood government benefits through adult transition and long-term estate planning.

$1.4M–$2.4M Estimated lifetime cost of supporting an autistic person (Harvard School of Public Health)
$70,000+ Average annual cost for a child with autism who also has intellectual disability
Only 1 in 5 Autism families have a formal long-term financial plan

Government Benefits: The Foundation

Before building any financial plan, families must understand the government safety net available to autistic individuals. These programs provide essential support — but they come with strict rules that affect how families can save and invest.

SSI — Supplemental Security Income

SSI provides monthly income to disabled individuals with limited income and resources. In 2024, the federal maximum is $943/month for an individual.

  • Eligibility: disability that meets SSA criteria + income/asset limits
  • Asset limit: $2,000 for individuals ($3,000 for couples)
  • Countable assets: bank accounts, savings, investments
  • NOT countable: primary home, one vehicle, burial funds, ABLE accounts (up to $100,000)
  • Apply at your local Social Security office or online at SSA.gov
  • Children can receive SSI; eligibility is re-evaluated at age 18
Medicaid

Medicaid is the backbone of healthcare and support services for autistic individuals across the lifespan. In New York, Medicaid covers ABA therapy, speech therapy, OT, PT, and much more.

  • SSI recipients automatically qualify for Medicaid in most states
  • NY has strong autism insurance law: commercial plans must cover autism services
  • OPWDD (Office for People With Developmental Disabilities) waiver provides NY Medicaid-funded community services for adults
  • Home and Community Based Services (HCBS) waivers fund supported employment, residential habilitation, respite, and more
SSDI — Social Security Disability Insurance

SSDI is for adults who have a work history or who have a parent who paid into Social Security. Unlike SSI, SSDI is not asset-tested.

  • Adult autistic children can receive benefits on a parent's Social Security record
  • The disability must have begun before age 22 (Childhood Disability Benefits — CDB)
  • Apply when the parent retires, becomes disabled, or dies
  • SSDI comes with Medicare coverage after a 2-year waiting period
Critical Rule: Receiving SSI means your family member's assets must stay below $2,000. Gifts, inheritances, and even well-meaning savings can disqualify them. This is where ABLE accounts and Special Needs Trusts become essential.

ABLE Accounts: Tax-Advantaged Savings That Don't Affect Benefits

The ABLE Act (Achieving a Better Life Experience Act) created a revolutionary savings vehicle: a tax-advantaged account that lets disabled individuals save money without losing their SSI or Medicaid eligibility.

Key ABLE Account Rules (2024)

  • Who qualifies: disability with onset before age 26 (expanding to age 46 in 2026)
  • Annual contribution limit: $18,000/year (from all sources combined)
  • Balance limit: Up to $100,000 is exempt from SSI asset count
  • Investment growth: Tax-free when used for qualified disability expenses
  • One account per person (though contributions can come from multiple sources)

What can ABLE funds pay for? Qualified Disability Expenses (QDEs) are broadly defined and include:

  • Housing, utilities, and home modifications
  • Transportation and vehicle modifications
  • Education, tutoring, and training programs
  • Health, wellness, and medical expenses
  • Assistive technology and communication devices
  • Employment training and job coaching
  • Financial management services
  • Personal support services
New York ABLE: NY uses the STABLE Account program (stableaccount.com). New York residents can also open accounts through any other state's ABLE program that accepts out-of-state residents. Shop programs by fees and investment options.

Special Needs Trusts: Protecting Larger Sums

When a family member, friend, or settlement will leave a significant sum for an autistic person, an ABLE account's contribution limits may not be enough. This is where a Special Needs Trust (SNT) comes in. A properly drafted SNT can hold unlimited funds without affecting SSI or Medicaid.

First-Party Special Needs Trust (d4A Trust)

Funded with the disabled person's own assets — typically from a personal injury settlement or inheritance paid directly to them.

  • Must be established before age 65
  • Must be established by parent, grandparent, legal guardian, or court
  • Medicaid payback provision required at death
Third-Party Special Needs Trust

Funded with assets from family members (typically parents and grandparents). This is the most common type for estate planning purposes.

  • No Medicaid payback requirement
  • Can be funded during life or at death (through a will or life insurance)
  • Remaining funds at death go to other beneficiaries named by the family
  • A trustee manages the funds and makes distributions for the beneficiary
Pooled Special Needs Trust

Managed by nonprofit organizations; individual sub-accounts are pooled for investment purposes. Good for smaller estates where professional management costs would be prohibitive.

  • NYSARC Trust Services is a well-known pooled SNT in New York
  • No minimum balance to join
  • Lower trustee fees due to pooled management

ABLE Account vs. Special Needs Trust: Which Do You Need?

Feature ABLE Account Special Needs Trust Regular Savings
Affects SSI? No (up to $100K balance) No Yes — disqualifying above $2,000
Contribution limit $18,000/year Unlimited Unlimited (but harmful)
Setup cost Free to low ($0–$50/yr) $1,000–$5,000 (attorney) Free
Who controls spending? Account holder (with some limits) Trustee Account holder
Tax advantages Tax-free growth & withdrawals Depends on trust structure None
Best for Day-to-day expenses, flexibility Large inheritances, estate planning Not recommended
Use both: Most families eventually use both an ABLE account (for flexibility and direct spending) AND a Special Needs Trust (for larger estate assets and inheritance). They work together — the trust can even contribute to the ABLE account.

The Letter of Intent: Your Family's Most Important Document

A Letter of Intent (LOI) is not a legal document, but it may be the most important thing you write. It is a comprehensive, personal guide for whoever will care for your family member after you are gone — a trustee, a sibling, a residential program, a guardian.

What to Include in a Letter of Intent

  • Daily routines: Wake time, meals, bedtime, sensory needs
  • Communication style: How they communicate, AAC devices used, favorite phrases
  • Medical information: Diagnoses, medications, allergies, physicians, insurance
  • Behavioral strategies: What calms them, known triggers, what works in a crisis
  • Support network: Friends, family, community connections
  • Preferences and passions: Interests, favorite foods, places they love
  • Goals and values: Your vision for their quality of life
  • Government benefits: SSI case number, Medicaid ID, OPWDD service contacts
  • Financial information: ABLE account, SNT trustee, bank contacts

Update the LOI every 1–2 years or after any major change. Share it with your attorney, trustee, and key family members.

Guardianship and Alternatives

When an autistic person turns 18, they are legally an adult. Parents lose automatic access to medical records, school information, and the ability to make decisions on their behalf. Many families assume legal guardianship is the only option — but there are important alternatives that preserve more autonomy.

Full Legal Guardianship (Article 17-A in NY)

A court process that transfers decision-making authority to a guardian. Best for individuals who genuinely cannot make their own decisions. Article 17-A is New York's specific guardianship statute for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities.

Drawback: It removes many legal rights from the individual. Courts increasingly require evidence that less-restrictive alternatives were considered first.

Supported Decision-Making (SDM)

The individual retains legal capacity but designates trusted supporters to help them understand information and make decisions. SDM Agreements are recognized in New York. See GRSCorp's Guardianship Alternatives Guide for New York.

Power of Attorney & Health Care Proxy

If an individual has capacity to understand and sign legal documents, they can grant a trusted person power of attorney (for financial matters) and designate a health care proxy (for medical decisions) — without losing any legal rights.

Financial Planning by Life Stage

Birth to School Age (0–5)
  • Apply for SSI if family income is low enough
  • Enroll in Medicaid / CHIP if not otherwise insured
  • Open an ABLE account — start small, build the habit
  • Update your life insurance; name an SNT as beneficiary (not the child directly)
  • Create a basic will with your attorney that includes a third-party SNT
  • Notify grandparents: gifts and inheritances must go to the SNT, never directly to a child on SSI
School Age (6–17)
  • Ensure IEP includes transition planning starting at age 15 (required by law)
  • Explore vocational assessment through school
  • Review SSI eligibility — family income rules are strict during childhood
  • Build ABLE account balance; use it for appropriate expenses
  • Contact OPWDD to begin eligibility determination (waiting lists are long — start early)
  • Consult a Special Needs Financial Planner to review your overall plan
Transition to Adulthood (18–21)
  • Apply for adult SSI at age 18 (re-evaluated on adult criteria, which are less strict)
  • Apply for SSDI on parent's record if parent is retired, disabled, or deceased
  • Pursue OPWDD eligibility and service planning
  • Explore guardianship vs. SDM — consult a special needs attorney
  • Apply for Power of Attorney and Health Care Proxy if appropriate
  • Explore supported employment, day habilitation, and residential options
  • Review and update your SNT and Letter of Intent
Adulthood (21+)
  • Maximize ABLE account contributions annually
  • Work with OPWDD service coordinator for community-based supports
  • Explore Self-Direction (managing your own OPWDD budget — see Self-Direction Budget Guide)
  • Review housing options: supported living, family home, group home, independent living
  • Annual review of all government benefits to ensure continued eligibility
  • Update estate plan and Letter of Intent every 2–3 years

Finding a Special Needs Financial Planner

General financial advisors often lack the specialized knowledge needed to navigate SSI rules, SNTs, and government benefit interactions. Look for a planner who has specific experience with special needs planning.

  • Ask about experience with Special Needs Trusts and SSI/Medicaid rules
  • Look for CFP (Certified Financial Planner) designation with special needs specialty
  • NAPFA (National Association of Personal Financial Advisors) can help find fee-only advisors
  • Special Needs Alliance (specialneedsalliance.org) has a directory of attorneys
  • Your local Arc chapter often has financial planning resources and referrals

New York-Specific Resources

OPWDD (Office for People With Developmental Disabilities)

NY's primary agency for adults with autism and developmental disabilities. Provides Medicaid-funded services including residential, day, employment, and family support. Early enrollment is critical — waiting lists exist. Start the process by age 15.

See: GRSCorp's OPWDD Navigation Toolkit

NY STABLE Account (ABLE Program)

New York's ABLE program. Open accounts at stableaccount.com. No residency requirement — NY residents can also use other states' ABLE programs. Compare fees and investment options before choosing.

NYSARC Trust Services

A pooled Special Needs Trust program for New York families. Managed by a nonprofit. Accepts accounts of all sizes with no minimum balance requirement. nysarc.org/services/trust

NY CDPAP (Consumer Directed Personal Assistance Program)

Allows Medicaid recipients to hire, train, and direct their own personal care attendants — including family members. See: GRSCorp's CDPAP Guide for New York

Start planning today. The most common regret among autism families who reach crisis is waiting too long to plan. You don't need to have everything figured out to take the first step — open an ABLE account, update your will, and begin the OPWDD application. Each step builds financial security for your child's future.
