How to Get Your Child Evaluated for Autism: A Step-by-Step Parent Guide

Published: May 31, 2026
Reading Time: 14 min
Topics: Autism Evaluation, Child Diagnosis, Parent Guide

If you suspect your child may be on the autism spectrum, knowing how to act — and where to start — can make the difference between years of waiting and getting your child the evaluation and support they need. This guide walks you through every step: recognizing early signs, requesting an evaluation, what happens during the assessment, and what to do after a diagnosis.

Early Signs by Age Group

Autism signs present differently at different developmental stages. Here is what to look for across age groups:

Babies & Toddlers (0–3 years)

Limited eye contact by 6 months. No babbling by 12 months. Not responding to their name by 12 months. Loss of previously acquired language or social skills. No words by 16 months, no two-word phrases by 24 months.

Preschool Age (3–5 years)

Difficulty with imaginative or pretend play. Playing in parallel rather than interactive play with peers. Echolalia (repeating words or phrases). Strong resistance to routine changes. Intense focus on specific objects or topics.

School Age (6–12 years)

Difficulty making or keeping friends. Sensory sensitivities that affect classroom performance. Meltdowns in response to transitions or unexpected changes. Challenges with unwritten social rules. Academic struggles despite apparent intelligence.

Teenagers (13–17 years)

Social isolation, extreme difficulty reading peers. Anxiety around social situations. Intense narrow interests that differ from peers. Difficulty with executive function (organization, planning). May have been "managing" but hitting limits of coping strategies.

When to Act

If your child is showing multiple signs from the lists above — particularly loss of language or social skills at any age — do not wait. Request an evaluation immediately. Earlier intervention consistently produces better outcomes. Trust your instincts as a parent.

Step 1 — Document What You Are Seeing

Before speaking with your pediatrician or requesting a formal evaluation, document your observations. A written record strengthens your case and helps evaluators understand your child's presentation over time.

What to document:

  • Specific behaviors you have observed, with dates and contexts
  • How often and how long behaviors occur
  • How your child responds to changes in routine, sensory input, or social situations
  • Any regression or loss of previously acquired skills
  • Teachers' or caregivers' observations (ask for these in writing)
  • Videos of behaviors at home — evaluators often find these invaluable

Step 2 — Talk to Your Pediatrician

Your pediatrician is usually the first professional stop in the evaluation process. Many use standardized developmental screening tools at regular well-child visits, but you do not have to wait for a scheduled visit.

1

Request a developmental screening

Ask specifically for a developmental and autism screening. The M-CHAT-R/F (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers) is commonly used for children 16–30 months. The Autism Spectrum Screening Questionnaire (ASSQ) or similar tools are used for older children.

2

Be specific about your concerns

Bring your written documentation. Describe behaviors precisely — "she lines up toys in a specific order and melts down if they are moved" is more useful than "she seems rigid." Specificity leads to appropriate referrals.

3

If your concerns are dismissed

You have the right to request a referral regardless of your pediatrician's opinion. If your concerns are dismissed and you remain concerned, seek a second opinion directly or contact your school district (see Step 3). "Wait and see" is not appropriate when autism is suspected.

Step 3 — Get a Referral to a Specialist

A formal autism evaluation is conducted by a specialist — not your general pediatrician. Ask for a referral to one of the following:

Developmental Pediatrician

A physician specializing in child development. Often the first specialist seen. Can diagnose and recommend treatment but typically does not perform the full neuropsychological battery.

Clinical Psychologist

Can conduct comprehensive psychological testing and provide an autism diagnosis. Many specialize in child assessment and can test for co-occurring conditions like ADHD and anxiety.

Neuropsychologist

Provides the most comprehensive evaluation, assessing cognitive, academic, executive, and behavioral functioning alongside autism screening. Best option when multiple conditions are suspected.

Don't Wait for a Referral — Use Two Pathways Simultaneously

While pursuing a medical evaluation, you can simultaneously request an evaluation through your child's school district at no cost to you. Under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), public schools are required to evaluate children suspected of having a disability that affects their education. These are separate processes and both are worth pursuing at the same time.

Step 4 — The Evaluation Process

A comprehensive autism evaluation is not a single test — it is a multi-component process that typically takes place over one to several sessions.

What a Comprehensive Evaluation Includes

  • Parent/caregiver interview: Detailed developmental history, pregnancy and birth, early milestones, family history, and current concerns.
  • Direct observation of the child: The evaluator observes your child in structured and unstructured settings, noting social interaction, communication, play, and behavioral patterns.
  • Standardized autism-specific tools: The ADOS-2 (Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule) is the gold standard observational tool. The ADI-R (Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised) is a detailed parent interview tool.
  • Cognitive and adaptive testing: IQ testing, language assessments, and adaptive behavior scales (how the child functions in daily life).
  • Behavioral and emotional assessments: Screening for co-occurring conditions including ADHD, anxiety, OCD, and learning disabilities.
  • Review of records: School reports, previous evaluations, and medical records.

How Long Does It Take?

A full evaluation typically takes 3–8 hours of assessment time spread over 1–3 sessions, plus time for the evaluator to score, interpret, and write the report. Expect 2–6 weeks from the final assessment session to receive the written report.

Step 5 — Understanding the Results

After the evaluation, you will receive a written report. This document is important — it will be used by schools, therapists, and healthcare providers. Here is how to read it.

Autism Spectrum Disorder — Diagnostic Levels

The DSM-5 categorizes ASD into three levels based on the amount of support required:

  • Level 1 — "Requiring support": Noticeable difficulties in social communication and flexible behavior without supports, but the person can generally function independently in many settings.
  • Level 2 — "Requiring substantial support": More marked difficulties in social communication and restricted/repetitive behaviors that are apparent even with supports in place.
  • Level 3 — "Requiring very substantial support": Severe deficits in social communication, marked inflexibility, and significant challenges in daily functioning.

Level does not determine your child's potential — it reflects their current support needs. These can change with appropriate intervention.

After the Diagnosis: Immediate Next Steps

1

Request an IEP evaluation from your school district

If your child is school age (or will be soon), submit a written request for a special education evaluation to your school district immediately. You do not need to wait. A private autism diagnosis is strong evidence to support this request.

2

Early intervention (children under 3)

If your child is under 3, contact your state's Early Intervention program. These federally funded services provide speech therapy, occupational therapy, and behavioral support at no or low cost and have the strongest evidence base for long-term outcomes.

3

Explore therapy options

Common supports for children with autism include Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA), speech-language therapy, occupational therapy (OT), social skills groups, and parent training. Your evaluator's recommendations will guide which are most appropriate.

4

Connect with the autism community

Parent support groups, local autism organizations, and online communities can be invaluable resources for practical advice, emotional support, and navigating systems.

Your Rights as a Parent

Key Legal Rights Every Parent Should Know

  • The right to request evaluation at any time: You do not need to wait for a teacher or doctor to suggest it. You can submit a written request to your school district directly.
  • The right to an Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE): If you disagree with the school's evaluation, you have the right to an IEE at the school district's expense.
  • The right to be involved in all IEP decisions: You are an equal member of the IEP team. No placement or service decision can be made without your participation and agreement.
  • The right to refuse consent: You must provide written consent before any evaluation or service begins. You can also revoke consent at any time.
  • The right to see all records: Under FERPA, you have the right to inspect and review any educational records related to your child.
  • The right to due process: If you disagree with the school's decisions, you can pursue mediation or a due process hearing.
