New York State Autism Services: Complete Guide for Families

New York has one of the most comprehensive autism service systems in the United States — but navigating it requires knowing which agency does what, when to apply, and how to advocate effectively. This guide covers the full continuum from birth through adulthood, specific to families in New York State.

The NYS Autism Service System: A Lifespan View

New York's autism service landscape is administered by multiple state agencies and the federal government. Understanding which agency is responsible at which stage of life is the key to accessing what your family needs.

Age Range Primary System Lead Agency Key Services
Birth – Age 3 Early Intervention (EI) NYS Dept. of Health / County Health Depts. Speech, OT, PT, Special Instruction, Family Support
Ages 3–5 Preschool Special Education (CPSE) School Districts / NYS Education Dept. Related services, special class, integrated settings
Ages 5–21 Special Education (CSE/IEP) School Districts / NYS Education Dept. IEP services, related services, ESY, transition planning
Age 18+ OPWDD / Medicaid / ACCES-VR OPWDD, OMH, Medicaid, ACCES-VR Housing, day programs, employment, therapy, respite
All Ages Insurance / Private Services DFS-regulated insurers ABA, speech, OT, psychiatric care (NYS Insurance Law §3221)

Early Intervention Program (Birth Through Age 3)

New York State's Early Intervention (EI) Program provides developmental services to children from birth to age 3 who have a developmental delay or disability — including autism. EI is one of the most powerful and time-sensitive programs available. It is provided at no cost to families regardless of income.

How EI Works in NYS

Referral: Any concerned parent, pediatrician, daycare provider, or other person can refer a child. Contact your county's Early Intervention Official (EIO) — found at health.ny.gov/community/infants_children/early_intervention. Referral must happen before the child's 3rd birthday.

Evaluation: A free multidisciplinary evaluation is conducted by EI-approved providers. If autism is suspected, evaluation typically includes a speech-language pathologist, developmental specialist, and psychologist.

IFSP: Eligible children receive an Individualized Family Service Plan (IFSP) — similar to an IEP but family-centered. Services are delivered in "natural environments" (home, daycare) whenever possible.

Services available: Speech-language therapy, occupational therapy, physical therapy, special instruction, ABA, family training, assistive technology, nutrition services, service coordination.

Don't Wait: If you have any developmental concern about a child under 3, call the Early Intervention hotline now: 1-800-522-5006. Referrals can come from anyone. There is no cost to request an evaluation. The earlier services begin, the better the outcomes.

Committee on Preschool Special Education (CPSE) — Ages 3–5

When a child turns 3, they "age out" of Early Intervention and transition to the Committee on Preschool Special Education (CPSE). This transition should begin planning at least 6 months before the 3rd birthday — don't wait until the last minute.

CPSE Key Facts

Who is Eligible?

Children ages 3–5 with a disability that adversely affects learning. Autism typically qualifies. Eligibility requires a school district evaluation and CPSE team determination.

What Services Can the CPSE Provide?

Related services (speech, OT, PT, ABA), special class placements (integrated or self-contained), integrated co-teaching settings, 12:1+1 or smaller class ratios, transportation.

Transition from EI to CPSE

Begin the CPSE referral process by the child's 2nd birthday if possible, and no later than 2.5 years. Your EI service coordinator should assist with transition planning. The CPSE must evaluate and develop an IEP before the child turns 3.

NYS-Approved Special Preschools

NYS has state-approved special education preschool programs operated by private voluntary agencies (e.g., AHRC NYC preschools, Eden II programs, various county BOCES). The CPSE can recommend placement at these programs when the school district cannot meet the child's needs.

Committee on Special Education (CSE) — School Age (5–21)

From kindergarten through age 21, autistic students' special education needs are served through the school district's Committee on Special Education (CSE). The CSE develops and maintains the IEP.

In NYS, the CSE must consider all placement options on the continuum, from full inclusion in general education with support services to special day schools and residential programs. Key principles:

  • Least Restrictive Environment (LRE): Students must be educated with non-disabled peers to the maximum extent appropriate.
  • Parental Participation: Parents are required IEP team members with equal standing.
  • Annual Review: IEPs must be reviewed and updated at least annually, with a triennial re-evaluation every 3 years.
  • Extended School Year (ESY): The CSE must consider ESY at every IEP meeting for students who may regress during breaks.
  • Transition Planning: Beginning at age 15 in NYS (age 16 under federal law), the IEP must include transition goals and services for life after school.

NYS-Specific IEP Placement Options

General education with supplementary services — Student in gen ed with IEP accommodations and related services.

Resource Room — 5–50% of the school day in a small group with a special education teacher for skill support.

Integrated Co-Teaching (ICT) — General and special education teacher co-teach in a blended classroom (up to 12 students with IEPs per class under NYS regs).

Special Class (6:1:1 or 12:1:1 or 8:1:1) — Small special education classes with paraprofessional support. Ratios indicate students:teacher:paraprofessional.

State-Approved Nonpublic Schools — When the district cannot meet the child's needs, placement at a private special education school at district expense.

Residential Schools — 24-hour educational and residential programs for the most intensive needs.

OPWDD — Office for People With Developmental Disabilities

OPWDD is the cornerstone of adult services for autistic New Yorkers with significant support needs. It funds and oversees services for individuals with developmental disabilities — including autism, intellectual disability, cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, and other IDDs — with onset before age 22.

OPWDD Eligibility

To qualify for OPWDD services, an individual must have:

  • A developmental disability (autism qualifies) diagnosed before age 22
  • Significant limitations in intellectual functioning (IQ substantially below average, OR functional limitations documented), AND
  • Substantial limitations in at least three of these adaptive skill areas: self-care, receptive/expressive language, learning, mobility, self-direction, capacity for independent living, economic self-sufficiency

Important: Not all autistic individuals qualify for OPWDD — those with average or above-average intellectual functioning and minimal adaptive limitations may not meet the threshold. These individuals may access services through other pathways (OMH, ACCES-VR, private insurance).

OPWDD Services Available

Residential Services

Group homes (Community Residences), Individualized Residential Alternatives (IRAs), supported apartments, and family care homes. Residential services are in very high demand — apply early and discuss waitlist strategies with your Care Manager.

Day Habilitation

Structured programs during daytime hours focused on developing and maintaining adaptive skills, community participation, and socialization. Can be center-based or community-based.

Community Habilitation

One-on-one support for skill development in the community — grocery shopping, transportation, banking, recreation, daily living skills. Flexible and individualized.

Supported Employment

Job coaching and employment support to help individuals obtain and maintain competitive integrated employment. OPWDD contracts with Employment Service Organizations (ESOs) across NYS.

Respite Services

Planned or emergency relief for family caregivers — in-home, overnight, or community-based. Respite is critically important for family sustainability and should be accessed proactively, not just in crisis.

Care Management

A Care Manager (from an OPWDD-enrolled voluntary agency) coordinates services, helps navigate the system, and advocates for the individual. Every OPWDD-enrolled individual is entitled to care management.

How to Apply for OPWDD

  1. Contact the OPWDD Developmental Disabilities Regional Office (DDRO) for your region. Five DDROs cover NYS: Metro NY (NYC/Long Island), Hudson Valley, Central, Finger Lakes, and Western.
  2. Submit a written request for eligibility determination. The DDRO will request documentation: diagnostic records, school records, psychological testing, functional assessments.
  3. OPWDD reviews submitted records and may conduct additional functional assessments to determine eligibility.
  4. If eligible, the DDRO issues a determination letter. The individual is enrolled in OPWDD and assigned to an Arc chapter or voluntary agency for Care Management.
  5. Work with your Care Manager to complete a Life Plan (OPWDD's person-centered planning document) and identify needed services.
  6. Services are arranged through OPWDD-enrolled providers. Note: Many services have waiting lists. Apply early — ideally by age 18, before the school system ends services at 21.

Medicaid Waiver Programs

New York State operates several Medicaid waiver programs that provide home and community-based services (HCBS) for individuals with disabilities, including autistic individuals. These programs allow Medicaid to fund services beyond standard medical care.

HCBS Waiver (OPWDD)

The primary Medicaid waiver for OPWDD-eligible individuals. Funds the services listed above (day hab, community hab, supported employment, respite, etc.). Enrollment is through OPWDD following eligibility determination.

Care at Home Waiver

Provides services to OPWDD-eligible children at home to prevent or delay out-of-home placement. Intended for children with significant medical or behavioral needs who would otherwise require institutional care.

Children's Waiver (NY State Plan)

Provides expanded Medicaid behavioral health services for children under 21, including ABA therapy when medically necessary. Children do not need OPWDD eligibility for this pathway — they need Medicaid and a qualifying diagnosis.

Medicaid Managed Care (MLTC / MAP)

Adults with disabilities enrolled in Medicaid managed care plans may access therapy, rehabilitation, and community supports. Plan networks vary — always verify that needed providers are in-network before switching plans.

SSI and Medicaid: Apply for Supplemental Security Income (SSI) and Medicaid at age 18 — when a child's own financial resources (not parents') are assessed. Most autistic young adults qualify for SSI based on disability, giving them Medicaid eligibility independent of family income. This Medicaid enrollment is a gateway to most adult services. Don't wait until the school exit date to apply.

ACCES-VR: Vocational Rehabilitation

Adult Career and Continuing Education Services — Vocational Rehabilitation (ACCES-VR) is New York State's vocational rehabilitation agency. ACCES-VR assists individuals with disabilities in obtaining and maintaining employment through a range of funded services.

What ACCES-VR Can Fund for Autistic Adults

  • Vocational assessment and planning
  • Job readiness training and skills development
  • Job placement services and employment support
  • Supported employment (job coaching)
  • Post-secondary education expenses (tuition, books, fees) when education leads to an employment goal
  • Assistive technology for workplace accommodations
  • Transportation to employment or training
  • Resume support, interview coaching, workplace relationship skills

ACCES-VR has district offices throughout NYS. Apply online at acces.nysed.gov/vr or contact your nearest district office. Eligibility requires a disability that creates a barrier to employment and a reasonable expectation that services will lead to an employment outcome. Many autistic adults — including those who are college-educated — qualify.

Parent Training & Information Centers (PTI)

Federally funded Parent Training and Information Centers provide free information, training, and advocacy support to parents of children with disabilities. New York State has two PTI centers:

Advocates for Children of New York (AFC)

Serves New York City families. Provides free legal advice, advocacy at IEP meetings, and workshops on special education rights. Known for strong legal advocacy for underserved families.

advocatesforchildren.org →

Resources for Children with Special Needs (RCSN)

Also serves NYC and surrounding counties. Provides information and referral, parent training, individual advocacy, and publications for families of children with disabilities.

resourcesnyc.org →

SPAN — Statewide Parent Advocacy Network

Statewide parent advocacy network (formerly EPAC) providing training, information, and connection to local parent support groups throughout NYS outside NYC.

spanadvocacy.org →

How to Navigate the NYS Autism System

The system is complex, but navigating it becomes more manageable with a clear understanding of the sequence and key decision points. Here is a rough timeline of the most important actions for families:

0–2
Birth – Age 2: Early Intervention If you have any developmental concern, call 1-800-522-5006 to refer for Early Intervention evaluation. Don't wait for the pediatrician to suggest it — parents can self-refer. Begin EI services as soon as eligible. Also begin the autism diagnostic evaluation process.
2.5
Age 2.5: Start CPSE Transition Planning Contact your school district to initiate CPSE referral. EI service coordinator should assist. CPSE evaluation must be completed and IEP in place before the child turns 3 — start early.
5
Age 5: Transition to CSE As child enters kindergarten, the Committee on Special Education (CSE) takes over from CPSE. Attend the transition IEP meeting, review the school's proposed program carefully, and advocate for appropriate placement and services.
14
Age 14: Begin Adult Planning Research Learn about OPWDD, SSI, Medicaid, and ACCES-VR now — before they are urgently needed. Contact OPWDD for information about eligibility. Begin connecting with adult providers.
15
Age 15: Transition Planning in IEP (Required) NYS requires IEPs to include transition planning by age 15 — post-secondary education goals, employment goals, independent living goals. The student themselves should be present and central to this planning. Apply to ACCES-VR now if vocational goals are relevant.
18
Age 18: Apply for SSI, Medicaid, and OPWDD At 18, the child's own financial resources (not parents') determine SSI and Medicaid eligibility. Apply immediately. Medicaid enrollment is the gateway to adult Medicaid-funded services. Begin OPWDD eligibility application. Consult a special needs estate attorney about establishing a Special Needs Trust.
21
Age 21: School Services End The student ages out of school-based services. All adult services (OPWDD, ACCES-VR, Medicaid) should already be in place. This transition should not feel like a cliff if planning began at 15–18.
